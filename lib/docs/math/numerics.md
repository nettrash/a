# Numerical methods

`a::math::numerics` solves the standard problems of numerical analysis on `f32` and `f64`. Everything is generic over the sealed trait `Real`, implemented for `f32` and `f64` only, and the linear algebra a method needs is that of [`a::math::matrix`](matrix.md): the LU decompositions of Newton's method for systems, of the stiff methods for differential equations and of the bases of the simplex method; the QR decompositions of Broyden's method and of the least-squares fits; the Hermitian eigenvalues of the Gaussian rules; and the singular values and eigenvalues of the AAA algorithm.

- Roots: the zeros of a function of one variable by bisection, Brent's method, the secant method and Newton's and Halley's methods, and of a system of equations by Newton's method with a Jacobian and by Broyden's method, both with a line search.
- Quadrature: the integral of a function of one variable by QUADPACK's adaptive Gauss–Kronrod quadrature (G10–K21 and G7–K15), by Clenshaw–Curtis and by tanh-sinh quadrature, over infinite intervals too; of a function of several variables by Genz and Malik's cubature, by Monte Carlo and by quasi-Monte Carlo on the Sobol' sequence; and the nodes and weights of the Gauss–Legendre, Gauss–Hermite, Gauss–Laguerre and Gauss–Jacobi rules.
- Differential equations: the initial value problem `y′ = f(t, y)` by the explicit methods `Rk4` (the classical Runge–Kutta method), `DormandPrince5` (Dormand and Prince's 5(4) pair) and `DormandPrince853` (their 8(5,3) method), and by the stiff methods `RadauIia` (Radau IIA of order 5) and `Bdf` (the backward differentiation formulas of orders 1 to 5), each controlling its own step size, with dense output and the location of events.
- Optimisation: the minimum of a function of one variable by the golden section and Brent's method; of several by Nelder and Mead's simplex, the conjugate gradient method and BFGS, and within bounds by L-BFGS-B; of a sum of squares by Gauss–Newton and Levenberg–Marquardt; and linear programming by the simplex method.
- Interpolation and approximation: the natural, clamped and not-a-knot cubic splines, Akima's spline and PCHIP, Chebyshev interpolation, barycentric interpolation, and rational approximation by the AAA algorithm; and differentiation by Richardson extrapolation. Exact derivatives by dual numbers come with `Dual<T>` in [#25](https://github.com/nettrash/a/issues/25).

Two rules hold throughout. A method with a tolerance never returns an answer it has not verified: where it cannot reach the tolerance it says so, by `None` (the methods for one equation, `solve`, `integrate` and `solve_at`, the derivatives), by a result with `converged` false and the best point or estimate it reached (the systems, the quadrature rules, the optimisers and the fits), by `OdeStatus::Failed` (`solve_ivp`), by `LpStatus::IterationLimit` or `None` (the simplex method), or by `converged()` false (AAA); inputs of the wrong shape panic. And a method that takes options comes with the defaults as `name` and with an options struct, built by `new()` and `with_*` calls, as `name_with`: `brent` and `brent_with`, `solve` and `solve_with`, `lbfgsb` and `lbfgsb_with`, `LinearProgram::solve` and `solve_with`. The quadrature rules other than `quad`, `monte_carlo` and `quasi_monte_carlo` take their `QuadOptions` as an argument.

## Examples

```rust
use a::math::matrix::Matrix;
use a::math::numerics::{
    LinearProgram, LpStatus, OdeOptions, RadauIia, RootOptions, Spline, bfgs, brent, brent_with,
    derivative, quad, solve_with,
};

// √2 as the root of x² − 2, with the default options and with a tolerance of one's own.
let r = brent(1.0, 2.0, |x: f64| x * x - 2.0).unwrap();
assert!((r - core::f64::consts::SQRT_2).abs() < 1e-15);
let loose = RootOptions::new().with_xtol(1e-6);
assert!((brent_with(1.0, 2.0, |x: f64| x * x - 2.0, &loose).unwrap() - r).abs() < 1e-6);

// π as an integral, with the error the rule estimated.
let i = quad(|x: f64| 4.0 / (1.0 + x * x), 0.0, 1.0).unwrap();
assert!(i.converged && (i.value - core::f64::consts::PI).abs() < 1e-15);

// The stiff decay y′ = −1000y over fifty time constants.
let f = |_t: f64, y: &Matrix<f64>, dy: &mut Matrix<f64>| dy[(0, 0)] = -1000.0 * y[(0, 0)];
let opts = OdeOptions::new().with_tolerances(1e-6, 1e-8);
let y = solve_with(f, &Matrix::from_rows(&[[1.0]]), 0.0, 0.05, RadauIia::new(), &opts).unwrap();
assert!((y[(0, 0)] - (-50.0f64).exp()).abs() < 1e-4);

// The slope of the sine at 1, to fourteen digits.
let d = derivative(|x: f64| x.sin(), 1.0).unwrap();
assert!((d - 1.0f64.cos()).abs() < 1e-14);

// Rosenbrock's valley, from (−1.2, 1), on the gradient alone.
let g = |x: &Matrix<f64>| {
    let (a, b) = (x[(0, 0)], x[(1, 0)]);
    (1.0 - a).powi(2) + 100.0 * (b - a * a).powi(2)
};
let grad = |x: &Matrix<f64>| {
    let (a, b) = (x[(0, 0)], x[(1, 0)]);
    Matrix::from_rows(&[[-2.0 * (1.0 - a) - 400.0 * a * (b - a * a)], [200.0 * (b - a * a)]])
};
let m = bfgs(g, grad, &Matrix::from_rows(&[[-1.2], [1.0]])).unwrap();
assert!(m.converged && (m.x[(0, 0)] - 1.0).abs() < 1e-5);

// The most of x + y with x + 2y ≤ 4, 3x + y ≤ 6 and x, y ≥ 0, at the vertex (1.6, 1.2).
let lp = LinearProgram::maximize(&[1.0f64, 1.0])
    .subject_to_le(&Matrix::from_rows(&[[1.0, 2.0], [3.0, 1.0]]), &[4.0, 6.0]);
let s = lp.solve().unwrap();
assert_eq!(s.status, LpStatus::Optimal);
assert!((s.objective - 2.8).abs() < 1e-12);

// And a cubic spline through twenty-one points of a curve.
let x: Vec<f64> = (0..=20).map(|i| i as f64 / 20.0).collect();
let y: Vec<f64> = x.iter().map(|&t| t.exp()).collect();
let s = Spline::cubic(&x, &y).unwrap();
assert!((s.evaluate(0.5).unwrap() - 0.5f64.exp()).abs() < 1e-5);
```

## Roots

| Function | Needs | Convergence | Guaranteed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `bisect`, `bisect_with` | a bracket `[a, b]`, `f(a)` and `f(b)` of opposite signs | linear, a bit an evaluation | yes |
| `brent`, `brent_with` | a bracket | superlinear (order about 1.84) where `f` is smooth | yes |
| `secant`, `secant_with` | two starting points | order 1.618 | no |
| `newton`, `newton_with` | `f` and `f′` | order 2 | no |
| `halley`, `halley_with` | `f`, `f′` and `f″` | order 3 | no |
| `newton_system`, `newton_system_with` | `f: Rⁿ → Rⁿ` and its Jacobian | order 2, with a line search | no |
| `broyden`, `broyden_with`, `broyden_with_jacobian` | `f` alone (or a first Jacobian) | superlinear, with a line search | no |

The five methods for one equation return `Option<T>`, and take their tolerances in a `RootOptions`: `xtol` and `rtol` on the root, `ftol` on the value, and `max_iter` (by default 0, 4ε, 0 and 512). None of them returns an estimate it has not verified. An estimate where `f` is exactly zero is returned at once. Otherwise, with `ftol` zero, `x` is returned when `f` changes sign within `tol(x) = xtol + rtol·|x|` of it (or within one float, where that is finer): a bracketing method has closed its bracket to that width, and an open method has taken a last step no longer than that and has found the sign change, against the estimate before or by evaluating `f` at `x ± tol(x)`. With `ftol` positive the value decides instead: an estimate is returned as soon as `|f(x)| ≤ ftol`, and never where it is larger. A sign change is all a method can verify, so it cannot tell a root from a pole or a jump — `1/(x − 0.3)` on `[0, 1]` gives 0.3 — and an `ftol` is what refuses a pole, where `|f|` grows without bound. A root of even multiplicity, where `f` touches zero without crossing, has no sign change and is returned only where `f` is exactly zero or, with an `ftol`, small enough. The answer is `None` when there is nothing to verify: no bracket, a NaN, an end or a start that is not finite, a tolerance that is negative or not finite, a vanishing derivative, a step that is not finite or no longer moves, or `max_iter` iterations.

`bisect` keeps a bracket in which the sign changes and halves it. A bracket within a factor of two is halved in length, as in the textbook, and so is any bracket when an absolute `xtol` can be reached by halving the length within as many steps as the float has bits. Otherwise — a bracket across zero or across many binades, under the default purely relative tolerance — halving the length would take a binade a step on the way to a root near zero (some 1,075 steps from `[−1, 2]` to `x = 0`), so the midpoint and the middle float of the bracket, the one that halves the count of floats in it, take turns. Any two bisections in a row then divide the count of floats by at least 1.5, which bounds the method at 220 iterations of an `f64` bracket and 110 of an `f32` one, inside the default limit; it stops at two neighbouring floats when no tolerance can separate them. The midpoint is formed so that it cannot overflow, and the ends may be given in either order.

`brent` is Brent's `zero` (R. P. Brent, *Algorithms for Minimization without Derivatives*, 1973, chapter 4), which is also the algorithm of `scipy.optimize.brentq`: the estimate `b` and the contrapoint `c`, on whose values the sign changes, are kept with `|f(b)| ≤ |f(c)|`; each step is the inverse quadratic interpolation through the last three points, or the secant through two, taken when it stays within three quarters of the bracket and is shorter than half the step before last, and the bisection otherwise; a step shorter than `δ = (xtol + rtol·|b|)/2` — `2ε·|b| + xtol/2` with the default `rtol` — is lengthened to `δ` towards the contrapoint; the method stops when `|c − b| ≤ 2δ`. The interpolation is Brent's, in ratios of the values, which does not overflow where `brentq`'s divided differences do. Brent's specification asks for a positive absolute tolerance; with one that halving the length can reach, the method is his unchanged. With a smaller one, as with the default `xtol` of zero at a root at zero, two safeguards of the bisection make it finish there: the bisections alternate the midpoint and the middle float as `bisect` does, and a bisection is forced whenever two steps in a row have not halved the count of floats in the bracket. The count then halves at least every six steps, which bounds the method at 385 iterations of an `f64` bracket and 193 of an `f32` one, inside the default limit of 512. That bound is not Brent's: with a positive `xtol` that halving reaches, where the method is his unchanged, his own bound is about `(k + 1)²` evaluations for the `k` bisections the tolerance needs, above the default limit in theory (the answer would be `None`); searches for the worst bracket found at most 249 evaluations at `xtol = 2·10⁻¹²`, 216 at `10⁻²⁰` and 206 at `10⁻³⁰⁰`. An infinite value at an end counts by its sign and is never interpolated through.

`secant` takes the chord through the last two estimates, `y − f(y)·(y − x)/(f(y) − f(x))`, its quotient of differences formed first so that neither underflows nor overflows before it must; `newton` the tangent, `x − f/f′`; and `halley` Newton's step `n = f/f′` corrected for the curvature, `n/(1 − n·f″/(2f′))`, which is `2f·f′/(2f′² − f·f″)` without its products of values, falling back to Newton's step where the correction would reverse it. A stationary point, where `f′` vanishes, gives `None`. None of the three accepts a small step on its own: after a wild step the secant iteration can fall back on a point where `f` is far from zero and stop moving there (`secant(0.1, 0.2, x¹⁰ − 1)` stopped on 0.2, where `f = −0.9999999`), which the sign change refuses.

`newton_system` solves the correction equation `J·δ = −f(x)` with the caller's Jacobian on `Matrix::lu`. `broyden` is Broyden's first ("good") method (C. G. Broyden, *A class of methods for solving nonlinear simultaneous equations*, Math. Comp. 19, 1965) in the form of Dennis and Schnabel (*Numerical Methods for Unconstrained Optimization and Nonlinear Equations*, 1983, algorithms A5.4.1, A8.3.2 and A3.4.1): a first Jacobian by forward differences (or the caller's, with `broyden_with_jacobian`), replaced after every step `s` that changed the residual by `y` by the nearest matrix mapping `s` to `y`, `B + (y − B·s)·sᵀ/(sᵀs)`, an update made in `O(n²)` on its QR factorisation by two sweeps of plane rotations. Both are globalised by Dennis and Schnabel's backtracking line search on `½‖f‖²` (A6.3.1: the full step first, then the minima of a quadratic and of cubic models of the merit, until it falls by `10⁻⁴` of what the linear model promises), which `SystemOptions::with_line_search(false)` turns off. `broyden` forms its approximation afresh — a restart — when it is singular, when its line search fails, after two steps in a row that the line search had to shorten, and before it believes any test of a root, so that no answer rests on an updated approximation or on a forward difference. A restart forms central differences and checks them, since a difference quotient can be wrong in its leading digit: a forward one where `f` curves within the step of `√ε` more than it rises, as near two roots closer than about `√ε` of their size, and a central one where the third derivative dominates within the step — where the step `√ε·|xₖ|` spans the scale on which `f` varies (`e^(x − 10⁹) − 2` from `10⁹`: a step of 15, and a quotient of `10⁵` for a slope of 1) and near a root of multiplicity three or more (`(x − 1)³` at `1 + 10⁻¹¹`: `2·10⁻¹⁶` for `3·10⁻²²`). Each column is formed again with a step a quarter as long, and the step is quartered, up to sixteen times, until the quotients at two steps agree entry by entry to a tenth, beyond the rounding of the values (`8ε` of each, over the step); where `f` is smooth the error then falls sixteenfold a quarter, and the longer step's column, accurate to about a tenth, is kept. Where rounding rather than the third derivative keeps two steps apart — it grows as the step shrinks, as in an equation whose terms of size one cancel — steps four and sixteen times longer are tried instead, and a zero after a quotient that was not zero is taken for a change of `f` below the spacing of its values, not for a derivative. A restart whose differences cannot be checked so ends the run, reported `Singular`; a restart costs at least `4n` evaluations. The confirming step of the residual test (below) solves on the approximation at the end of its step, so `broyden` takes it only within the steps over which the differences were seen to hold.

The system solvers return a `SystemResult` — the point, the residual there and its infinity norm, the counts of steps, evaluations and Jacobians, `converged`, and a `SystemStop` that says why they stopped — or `None` for a start or a first residual that is not finite or for invalid options; inputs of the wrong shape panic. A point is a root by one of three tests, each independent of the units of `x` and of `f`. **Step**: the Newton correction `δ` changes no unknown by more than `xtol` (4ε) of itself; the step is then taken. **Residual**: `f(x)` exactly zero, `‖f(x)‖∞ ≤ ftol` when the caller gives one, or the residual down to rounding and one more step to prove it. The residual is down to rounding when every `|fᵢ(x)| ≤ 8ε·(|J|·|x|)ᵢ`, the backward error of `x` as a root of the system with its Jacobian perturbed by `8ε`, and `δ` is below `ε^(2/3)` of every unknown; neither is enough alone, since the size of the linear terms overstates the rounding of an equation whose terms cancel or that `f` evaluates exactly (`0.01·(x₁ − 10¹⁶)` at `x₁ = 10¹⁶`), and near a nearly double root a short correction is not a small error. So `δ` is taken, to `y`, and the next correction is solved for on the same factors, `δ̄ = −J⁻¹·f(y)`, the simplified Newton correction of Deuflhard's natural monotonicity test (*Newton Methods for Nonlinear Problems*, 2004): `y` is a root when `δ̄` is below `xtol` of every unknown, and while `δ̄` is shorter than `δ` the iteration goes on from `y`. When it is no shorter the iteration has stopped converging — at the floor of the rounding of `f`, or because Newton's step overshoots where the size of the linear terms was a loose bound (`arctan(x₂ − 10¹²)` coupled to `x₁ = 10¹⁶` by `x₁ − 10¹⁶`: from `x₂ = 10¹² + 2` the step lands at `10¹² − 3.5`) — and that is no proof of a root: the rounding test decides, by the line search along `δ`, with the line search on or off (off, the iteration goes on from the point the search found, if it found one). **Rounding**: the line search can lower the residual no further along a correction `δ` below `ε^(2/3)` of every unknown, and `f` is seen to follow its linear model along `δ` at longer steps. To first order `f(x + λδ) = (1 − λ)·f(x)`, and the search would succeed; what stops it is a deviation from that model as large as `f(x)`, which is either the rounding of `f` (as at the root of the trigonometric system, whose terms cancel to 10⁻¹⁶) and of the points to floats, or the shape of `f`: a local minimum of `‖f‖` that is no root, where the floats of an unknown are too coarse for the steps that would find a lower value (`(u − 1/3)² + 1` at `x = 10¹⁴ + u`, where the floats are 1/64 apart), or a kink. A rounding does not grow with the step and a curvature grows as its square, so `f` is evaluated at `x + kδ`, `k = 4, 16, …, 4096`, and the point is a root to rounding when, at two successive `k`, every equation is within a sixteenth of its linear model, measured by the smaller of the change the model predicts for the residual and the size of the equation's own terms along the step: `|fᵢ(x + kδ) − (1 − k)fᵢ(x)| ≤ (k − 1)·min(‖f(x)‖∞, Σⱼ|Jᵢⱼδⱼ|)/16`. A curvature as large as `f(x)` at `δ` is `k²` times as large at `kδ` and fails at every `k`; each of the two sizes alone can be fooled in a system (terms that cancel along the step, as in the equation of a circle that a line passes closely; an equation whose residual is far below another's at its rounding), and for one equation both are `|f(x)|`, so that a step that passes is a proof of a root between `x` and `x + kδ`, where `f` has the opposite sign. For a system the test is a check of the model, not a proof, and, like the line search, its first size compares the equations with one another, so that it depends on how they are scaled against each other, though not on the units of `f`. Where `f` does not follow its model the method stops with `Rounding` and `converged` false, at its best point (so at the local minimum above, and at the kink `|x − 1| + 10⁻¹²`). Short of an `ftol`, an answer therefore has a Newton correction below `xtol` or `ε^(2/3)` of every unknown — to first order it is that close to a root of `f` as evaluated, and at a regular root with `f` evaluated accurately the step taken after the test leaves it within a few roundings — and where the rounding of `f` itself leaves the root less certain, as in an ill-conditioned problem, it is as near as that rounding allows. The step and residual tests are of the first order, which has three limits, stated under **Limits** with what was measured: a root of multiplicity `m`, where the correction understates the error by about `m`; a local minimum of `|f|` that is no root but shallower than `f″·(xtol·|x|)²/2`, which a tolerance on the step cannot tell from a double root; and the resolution of the format, where `xtol·|xᵢ|` is not small against the scale on which `f` varies. Otherwise the method stops with `converged` false and the best point it reached: on a singular or non-finite Jacobian, which is never divided by, on a line search that cannot lower the merit (a local minimum of `‖f‖` that is not a root), or after `max_iter` (100) steps.

```rust
use a::math::matrix::Matrix;
use a::math::numerics::{RootOptions, SystemStop, bisect, brent, brent_with, broyden, newton_system};

// A root at zero, where no relative tolerance can be met: both bracketing methods end on zero or
// on the float next to the sign change, with the default options.
assert!(bisect(-1.0, 2.0, |x: f64| x * x * x).unwrap().abs() < 1e-300);
assert!(brent(-1.0, 2.0, |x: f64| x.cbrt()).unwrap().abs() < 1e-300);

// A pole is a sign change too; a tolerance on the value refuses it.
let pole = |x: f64| 1.0 / (x - 0.3);
assert!((brent(0.0, 1.0, pole).unwrap() - 0.3).abs() < 1e-15);
assert!(brent_with(0.0, 1.0, pole, &RootOptions::new().with_ftol(1e-8)).is_none());

// Rosenbrock's function as a system, 10(y − x²) = 0 and 1 − x = 0, from the classic start.
let f = |v: &Matrix<f64>| {
    let (x, y) = (v[(0, 0)], v[(1, 0)]);
    Matrix::from_column_major(2, 1, vec![10.0 * (y - x * x), 1.0 - x])
};
let j = |v: &Matrix<f64>| Matrix::from_rows(&[[-20.0 * v[(0, 0)], 10.0], [-1.0, 0.0]]);
let x0 = Matrix::from_column_major(2, 1, vec![-1.2, 1.0]);
let r = newton_system(&x0, f, j).unwrap();
assert!(r.converged && r.stop == SystemStop::Residual && r.x.as_slice() == [1.0, 1.0]);
let b = broyden(&x0, f).unwrap();
assert!(b.converged && b.x.as_slice().iter().all(|&v| (v - 1.0).abs() < 1e-15));

// A system with no real solution is reported, with the best point reached.
let g = |v: &Matrix<f64>| {
    let (x, y) = (v[(0, 0)], v[(1, 0)]);
    Matrix::from_column_major(2, 1, vec![x * x + y * y + 1.0, x - y])
};
let none = broyden(&Matrix::from_column_major(2, 1, vec![1.0, 2.0]), g).unwrap();
assert!(!none.converged && none.residual >= 1.0);
```

### Measured

Every number here comes from the tests in `lib/tests/math/numerics/root.rs` and `system.rs`, which assert them, or from the scripts named; the references are SciPy 1.18 and mpmath 1.3, run by `lib/tests/math/numerics/data/gen_roots.py` and `gen_systems.py`, whose output is the tables in the tests. The larger random runs, the search for the worst bracket, the battery of 1,800 runs of the systems and its `f32` runs were made with probes outside the crate, and are said to be.

**Brent against `brentq`**, on 63 brackets — the problems of Alefeld, Potra and Shi and hard cases: multiple roots, roots at zero, flat functions, a dynamic range of 10⁶⁰⁰, the largest and the smallest floats, poles, jumps and kinks. At SciPy's tolerances (`xtol` 2·10⁻¹², `rtol` 4ε) `brent` takes exactly the steps of `brentq` on 57 of them, and of a model of the algorithm in Python on all 63. Of the other six, five take fewer evaluations here (`x − 10³⁰⁸` on `[0, MAX]`: 4 against 53, where `brentq`'s interpolation overflows; `eˣ − e⁻⁶⁰⁰` on `[−700, 700]`: 26 against 34; `[−MAX, MAX]`, which `brentq` refuses: 6; `x² − 2` on `[0, 10²⁰]`, where the safeguarded bisection acts: 33 against 126; a root at the first end: 1 against 2), and on one rounding in the two forms of the interpolation decides a step differently (12 against 11). Both answers always lie within the tolerance of a sign change. The model and `brentq` ran on aarch64 macOS, and 25 of the functions call the libm (cos, sin, tan, exp, pow, cbrt), whose last bits differ between platforms and can decide the last steps; so the tests compare the steps on the model's values, replayed at every point it evaluated (`data/roots_libm.txt`), and judge the answers with the platform's own libm too. With glibc's on x86_64 two brackets go otherwise: `cos x − x` takes 8 evaluations rather than 10 at the defaults, to an answer an ulp away, and `∛x` at SciPy's tolerances takes the same 33 to an answer 396 ulps away (−2.8·10⁻¹³, for the root 0). At the default tolerances against `brentq` at the least `xtol` it accepts (5·10⁻³²⁴):

**Bisection** takes 52 iterations from `[1, 2]` to 4ε, and at most 75 (`f64`, 200,000 random brackets at two tolerances) and 40 (`f32`, 200,000) on brackets whose ends and roots range from the subnormals to 10³⁰⁸ and include zero; the bound is 220 and 110.

**Random brackets**: 4,000 brackets of ten families (linear, cubic, `tanh`, exponential, triple root, square-root kink, rational, kinked, sine, quintic), scaled by `10^±20`, ends in either order, one in seven with the root at zero: every answer of both methods lies inside its bracket and within its tolerance of a sign change of the same float function, judged by evaluating it at every float within the tolerance. `brent` averages 3.5 to 11.6 evaluations on the smooth simple roots and 100 on the triple root, bisection about 55; at most 136 for `brent` on brackets spanning every magnitude, and 206 on the worst bracket a search for one has found (a power 16.4 on one side of the root, linear on the other), against the bound of 387 evaluations (385 iterations and the two ends). 2,000 `f32` brackets: the same.

### Limits

At a multiple root Brent's method converges only linearly, and needs 100 to 206 evaluations at the default tolerance (the bound of its safeguards is 387). The open methods cannot meet a relative tolerance at a root at zero that they approach only linearly (a multiple root, as `newton` on `x²` from 1, which halves its estimate each step): give them an `ftol`, or for a root of odd multiplicity, where `f` changes sign, an `xtol`. A root of a system at which the Jacobian is singular is approached only linearly, and a root at the origin cannot pass a relative step test, so Powell's singular system is reported unverified although its residual falls to 10⁻⁶⁰ (`newton_system`; `broyden` stops at about 10⁻³³, where its approximation is singular to rounding, with a stop that depends on the platform, as above): an `ftol` in the units of `f` solves it (`ftol = 10⁻²⁰`: both methods). Unknowns whose root is zero beside others that are not are approached until they are subnormal or the residual vanishes, as on the helical valley. The rounding test is a proof for one equation and a check of the linear model for a system: it presumes that no feature of `f` between the length of the correction and 4096 times it mimics the model at two steps in every equation (none was found on the probes under **Measured**). A kink fails it (`|x − 1| + 10⁻¹²` ends `Rounding` unconverged at 1). The line search does not make Newton's method converge from everywhere, and on some problems it does worse than none: it can end at a local minimum of `‖f‖`, as on the trigonometric system from ten times its start; on Wood's function `newton_system` with it fails from a hundred times the start (after the default 100 steps; after 652, with a failed line search, given 1,000) and from 7 of the 15 starts of the battery, from all of which plain Newton converges; `broyden` converges on Wood's function from 7 of the 15 and on the variably dimensioned function of order 30 from 1 (`newton_system` from all 15). Every such failure is reported, with the best point. `broyden` fails on Brown's function of order 30 from its start, whose last row of the Jacobian, entries of 2·10⁻⁹ against values of 1, differences with a step of `√ε` cannot resolve, forward or central; `newton_system` solves it, and `broyden_with_jacobian` takes a first Jacobian from the caller. `broyden`'s checked restarts cost at least `4n` evaluations, and on the 580 runs of the battery that it and the first version both converge on it takes 126 % more evaluations than that version (131,648 against 58,124), which could stop on points that were no roots. `newton_system` trusts the caller's Jacobian: one wrong in its leading digit can make its tests accept a point that is no root. `broyden` checks its differences, and where it cannot — an unknown whose floats are further apart than the scale on which `f` varies in it, or a column that rounding keeps from agreeing at every step tried — it reports `Singular` with its best point; within a few roundings of a root of multiplicity three or more it often cannot (from the 1,000 starts above it converges on 81 % for `m = 3` and 20 % for `m = 7`), and on a double root coupled to an unknown of 10¹⁶ it ends unconverged after its 100 steps, `3.6·10⁻¹²` and `5.5·10⁻¹²` of the root from it (from `x₂ = 3` and `2.5`), where `newton_system` converges. The step and residual tests are of the first order, and accept a point that is no root, or one further from a root than the tolerance, where `f` curves within the distances they rest on, `xtol·|xᵢ|` and, for the bound of the residual test, `8ε·|xᵢ|`. Three limits were measured. At a root where the Jacobian is singular the correction understates the error by about the multiplicity `m`, and the confirming step accepts a point up to `m/(1 − 1/m)^(m−1)·xtol` from the root (4 at `m = 2`, 6.75 at 3, 17.6 at 7); measured, `2(m − 1)·xtol` on `(x − 1)ᵐ` for `m` from 2 to 7 in `f64` and `f32`, and `4·xtol` on the coupled double root. A local minimum of `|f|` that is no root but whose least value `c` is below about `f″·(xtol·|x|)²/2` cannot be told from a double root (which it is of `f − c`) by a tolerance on the step: on `(u − 1/3)² + c` at `x = T + u`, `c` from 10⁻⁴ to 1, from four starts by both methods with and without the line search (`T` from 10¹⁰ to 10¹⁶ in tenths of a decade in `f64`, 10² to 10⁷ in `f32`; probes), the step test accepted such points up to 0.8 times that bound (`f32`: 0.7) and the residual test up to 3.2 times (2.8): in `f64` from `T = 6.3·10¹²` for `c = 10⁻⁴`, `6.3·10¹³` for 10⁻² and `6.3·10¹⁴` for 1, in `f32` from `1.3·10⁴`, `1.3·10⁵` and `1.3·10⁶`; four systems of two equations without a root whose least `‖f‖` is as small (a local minimum of `|f₁|` beside a line, alone and coupled, a line passing the unit circle closely, `uv + c` on `u = v`) gave such points from `T = 10¹²`. And where an unknown is so large that `xtol·|xᵢ|` is not small against the scale on which `f` varies in it, at the resolution of the format, the tests can accept points far from a root: on `u³ − 2u − 5` and `u³ − 2u + 2` at `x = T + u`, which have a root and a local minimum of `|f|` a unit or so apart, from five starts in the four ways, every converged answer was within 0.47 times `xtol·|x|` of the root up to `T = 2.5·10¹⁴`, and from `T = 3.2·10¹⁴`, where `xtol·|x| = 0.28`, the tests accepted points up to 10.8 times it from the root (`u = 1.25` for the root −1.77, by `newton_system` without the line search; in `f32` from `T = 10⁶`, up to 7.7); on `u³ − 1`, whose root is the only point where `|f|` is small, answers lie up to 2.7 times `xtol·|x|` from the root at `T = 10¹⁵` (`newton_system`; `broyden` 0.7), 0.35 at `10¹⁴`, and in `f32` up to 2.5 at `3·10⁶` and 1.2 at `10⁶`, 0.33 at `10⁵`. `quad_g10k21` and `quad_g7k15` follow QAGS step for step (Piessens, de Doncker-Kapenga, Überhuber and Kahaner, *QUADPACK*, 1983): the interval with the largest error estimate is bisected; the error of an interval is `|K − G|` scaled by `resasc = ∫|f − mean|` as `resasc·min(1, (200|K − G|/resasc)^1.5)` and never below `50ε` times `∫|f|` over it; bisections that leave the estimate where it was while the error does not fall end the integration for round-off; and Wynn's epsilon algorithm extrapolates the sequence of estimates as the bisection homes in on a singular end, with QUADPACK's test for a divergent integral. The departures from QUADPACK are all towards honesty. No interval is bisected so far (2048 ulps, in the rule's variable and in the integrand's type) that its nodes could round onto its ends. The totals of the estimates, of their errors and of `∫|f|` are summed afresh over the intervals after every bisection: QUADPACK keeps running sums, and on `∫₀^∞ e^(−(x−40)²) dx` they cancel — the first panel of QAGI sees the bump at one node, with an error of 86, its halves see nothing, and `86 + 2·10⁻⁵⁶ − 86 = 0` is taken for the error of the whole: QUADPACK and `scipy.integrate.quad` return `8.3·10⁻⁵⁶`, error 0, for `√π`. A value of a bisected interval that the nodes of the half it lies in do not account for (neither node beside it a tenth as large) is evidence of a feature between them, and stays in the error of that half, and of its halves, as its share of the parent's `∫|f|`, until a node beside it sees a tenth of it. Every sample of the bisected interval is offered to the half on its side, not only the largest: with peaks of width `3·10⁻⁴` about 0.0643 and 0.2499 on `[0, 1]`, `[0, 0.5]` saw the first at 0.30 of its height and the second at 0.84 at its centre, the largest sample of both its sides, and, offered only that, the left half had no evidence of the first peak and its nodes missed it: `quad_g7k15` returned `4.76·10⁻⁴ ± 7.8·10⁻¹⁴` at `10⁻¹²` for `1.008·10⁻³`. An interval keeps up to four such values, the heaviest, so that one feature's evidence does not push out another's. The value at the centre of a bisected interval lies at an end of both halves and is evidence for each: what it can hide there is its value times the gap between that end and the half's outermost node, a gap that halves with each bisection towards the end. QUADPACK takes either half's nodes for the centre's, and so a peak of width 0.001 about −0.0021 on `[−1, 1]`, which the centre sees as 0.012 and the right half's nearest node as `10⁻⁸`, loses the tail between to every estimate: `quad_g7k15` and `scipy.integrate.quad` come out `1.5·10⁻³` off relatively with the error `2.8·10⁻¹⁴`. Four copies of the extrapolation, fed the estimates moved by their rounding `ε∫|f|` with random signs, measure how far the epsilon algorithm magnifies that rounding, which its own error estimate does not see. QUADPACK's floor `50ε∫|f|` on the error of an interval is kept where `∫|f|` is below `tiny/(50ε)`, as `50ε·tiny`. And a result whose error estimate is a tenth of the result or more is not accepted unless the error is at the round-off floor `100ε∫|f|`. Every error estimate here is the difference of a finer and a coarser estimate, trusted because the finer is far the better; where the nodes have seen only the tail of a feature the coarser holds anything from none to most of what the finer holds, and for a positive integrand the difference is then always below the finer estimate, so that a test against the whole estimate lets it through. QUADPACK accepts such an estimate whenever the absolute tolerance allows, and returns `5.5·10⁻⁷` with the error `9.9·10⁻⁷` for `∫₀^∞ e^(−(x−30)²) dx = √π` at `epsabs = 10⁻⁶`, and `1.0·10⁻⁹ ± 5.2·10⁻¹⁰` from QAGI's first panel for a Lorentzian of width `1.3·10⁻⁴` about 77.2 on `[−3, ∞)` (`4.1·10⁻⁴`) at `10⁻⁹`. An infinite interval is mapped onto `(0, 1]` as QAGI maps it, `x = a + (1 − t)/t` (`x = b − (1 − t)/t` for `(−∞, b]`, the two halves of the line folded together), and integrated with the 15-point pair whichever pair was asked for; its infinite end is then a singular end at `t = 0` for the extrapolation.

`clenshaw_curtis` is adaptive in degree and composite. A panel holds the integrand at the Chebyshev–Lobatto points `cos(kπ/N)`, `N = 32, 64, 128, 256`, each level evaluating only the points the level before lacks, with the weights of the interpolant integrated exactly (the cosine series in Trefethen's "Is Gauss quadrature better than Clenshaw–Curtis?", 2008; the two end weights in their closed form `1/(N² − 1)`). Its error estimate asks for two successive agreements — the larger of `|I_N − I_{N/2}|` and `|I_{N/2} − I_{N/4}|`, enlarged by `r/(1 − r)` where they fall slowly — and each panel is checked at two points off its grid against its interpolant, Chebfun's sample test, which catches an integrand that aliases on every level (`T₆₄` is 1 at all 33 points and at the 17 and 9 among them). To it is added what the rounding of the points could move the sum by, which every level shares and no difference of levels sees: a point is formed in `f64` as `mid + half·cos(kπ/N)`, up to about `ε max(|a|, |b|)` off, which counts in full against the variation of the integrand between neighbouring points; an `f32` integrand is called at the point rounded to `f32`, by an amount known at each point and as likely up as down, which counts as four standard deviations of a sum of independent errors. The panel of the largest error is refined: doubled, or at 257 points bisected into two panels of 33 that reuse its three values at their ends. A value of the bisected panel that the points of the half it lies in do not account for stays with the half, as in QAGS, as its share of the panel's `∫|f|`, until a point beside it sees a tenth of it, up to four values a half, the heaviest: a panel that saw a peak at one point was replaced by halves of 33 points that missed it, and `∫₀¹ e^(−((x−0.286)/0.001)²) dx` came out `9.9·10⁻⁶⁰` within its estimate after a point had seen 0.95; and a half that kept only the heaviest value lost the second of two peaks of width `3·10⁻⁴` about 0.160 and 0.303 that the panel had seen at 0.98 of its height (`5.3·10⁻⁴ ± 2.8·10⁻¹⁰`, converged, at `10⁻⁸`, for `8.5·10⁻⁴`). A bisected panel keeps its own estimate where that is better than its halves', and the errors of the panels are summed. As in QAGS, a result whose error estimate is a tenth of it or more is not accepted unless the error is at the round-off of the sums. The rule evaluates the integrand at the ends of the interval.

`tanh_sinh` substitutes `x = c + h·tanh((π/2) sinh t)` and takes the trapezoidal rule in `t`, the step halving from 1 and each level evaluating only its new nodes (Takahasi and Mori 1974). Every node is formed from its distance to the nearer end, `h·2q/(1 + q)` with `q = e^(−2u)` and `u = (π/2) sinh |t|`, so the nodes come as close to an end at 0 as the smallest normal number, to a nonzero end within an ulp of it, and none is evaluated where it would round onto an end: an integrand infinite or NaN exactly at an end is welcome. An interval with no number of the type at its centre but its ends is not evaluated at all, and one whose half-width is below the smallest normal number, which has no node but its centre, ends after it; both unconverged. Every level is summed out to its last node distinct from the ends. The error estimate is the difference of the last two levels while the convergence is double exponential (a difference at most the 1.5th power of the one before, relatively to `∫|f|`, two levels running, from a difference below `∫|f|`), and otherwise the larger of the last two differences times the geometric tail `r/(1 − r)`; to it are added the mass beyond the outermost node of each side, from a power law through its two outermost values, the round-off of the sum, and what the rounding of the nodes could move it by — a node formed in `f64` from the end it approaches, `x = b − d`, is up to about `ε(|x| + d)` off, counted in full against the variation of the integrand between neighbouring nodes (a Gaussian of width 0.061 about 21.4 on `[10, ∞)` came out `1.5·10⁻¹⁵` off with the estimate `4.3·10⁻¹⁶` without it), and the rounding of the node to `f32` for an `f32` integrand as four standard deviations of independent errors. No result is accepted before the third level, nor while the levels move apart, nor, as in QAGS, with an error estimate a tenth of the result or more unless the error is at the round-off of the sums: levels that disagree in their first digit have only grazed a feature, and `1.1·10⁻⁸ ± 1.1·10⁻⁸` was accepted for `∫₋₁¹ e^(−((x+0.04)/0.01)²) dx = 0.0177` at `10⁻⁶`. On `[a, ∞)` the map is exp-sinh, `x = a + e^((π/2) sinh t)`, on the line sinh-sinh, `x = sinh((π/2) sinh t)`, and an infinite side is summed until its terms die away.

`cubature`, on a box of two dimensions or more, uses Genz and Malik's embedded rules of degrees 7 and 5 on `2^d + 2d² + 2d + 1` points (Genz and Malik 1980), halves the box of the largest error along the axis of the largest fourth difference, and evaluates each box once. Its error estimate comes from three null rules on the same points, of degrees 5 (`I₇ − I₅`), 3 and 1, in the manner of Berntsen, Espelid and Genz's DCUHRE (1991): where they fall off with their degree the estimate is ten times `|I₇ − I₅|`, where they do not ten times the largest of them. As in the one-dimensional rules, a result is accepted only with an error estimate below a tenth of it, or at the round-off of the sums: a Gaussian of width 0.03 about `(0.7, 0.3)` in the unit square was accepted on its first box as `1.0·10⁻²⁰ ± 9.0·10⁻²⁰`, for `2.8·10⁻³`. In one dimension `cubature` is `quad_g7k15`.

`monte_carlo` averages the integrand at points drawn uniformly from the open box by xoshiro256** (Blackman and Vigna 2021), seeded through SplitMix64, and reports the standard error of the mean from the sample variance (Welford's update, in `f64` whatever the type); a coordinate the type rounds onto a face is drawn again. `quasi_monte_carlo` averages over the Sobol' sequence — Joe and Kuo's direction numbers (2008) for `SOBOL_MAX_DIMENSION` = 1024 dimensions, Antonov and Saleev's Gray-code order — shifted by `R` random vectors modulo 1 (Cranley and Patterson 1976, `R = 8` by default): each shifted estimate is unbiased, and the error is the standard error of their mean, with `R − 1` degrees of freedom. `MonteCarloOptions` holds the number of samples, the seed and `R`. The direction numbers are Joe and Kuo's table new-joe-kuo-6.21201, Copyright (c) 2008, Frances Y. Kuo and Stephen Joe, redistributed under their BSD-style licence, whose full text and disclaimer of warranty are at <https://web.maths.unsw.edu.au/~fkuo/sobol/>.

`QuadOptions` holds `epsabs` and `epsrel` (`10⁻¹²` for `f64`, `10⁻⁵` for `f32`), `max_subintervals` (200 intervals, panels or boxes) and `max_evaluations` (100,000), which no rule exceeds; a result is converged only if its error estimate is at most `max(epsabs, epsrel·|value|)` — necessary, not sufficient, for every rule also asks of an estimate that it has resolved the integrand, an error below a tenth of it or at the round-off of the sums — and never when the value or the error is not finite in the integrand's type (an `f32` integral above `f32::MAX` comes back infinite, not converged). A result the rule could not bring within its tolerances comes back with `converged` false, its best estimate and its error estimate, and an integrand that returns a NaN or an infinity where it is evaluated ends the integration there, not converged. `None` is for a NaN limit, for options that ask for nothing (both tolerances zero, one negative or NaN, no room for one application of the rule) and for an infinite limit given to a rule that takes none. `a = b` is the integral 0 and `a > b` the negated integral. For the Monte Carlo rules `converged` says only that the estimate and its error are finite numbers.

### Gaussian rules

`legendre`, `hermite`, `laguerre` and `jacobi` return the `n` nodes and weights of Gauss–Legendre, Gauss–Hermite, Gauss–Laguerre and Gauss–Jacobi quadrature, and `hermite_scaled` and `laguerre_scaled` the same nodes with the weights `wᵢ e^(xᵢ²)` and `wᵢ e^(xᵢ)`. Up to `GOLUB_WELSCH_LARGEST` = 100 points the nodes are the eigenvalues of the Jacobi matrix (Golub and Welsch 1969), from `Matrix::hermitian_eigenvalues`. Above it the zeros of Legendre's, Hermite's and Laguerre's polynomials are found by Newton's method on the three-term recurrence from asymptotic first guesses — Olver's expansion in the zeros of `J₀` near the ends and Tricomi's in the interior for Legendre (the guesses of Hale and Townsend 2013), the Airy-zero form of Langer's approximation for Hermite, both for Laguerre — in `O(n²)` work and `O(n)` memory, nothing of size `n²` formed; the zeros found must be distinct and in order, or the rule falls back to the eigenvalues. Either way every node is finished by Newton's method in `f64` and then in double-double, and its weight is the Christoffel number `μ₀/Σₖ pₖ(x)²` of the recurrence at the node — the same number as the derivative formula, computed without the derivative — the polynomials rescaled by powers of two as they grow so that nothing overflows or underflows on the way: a weight too small for the type comes out subnormal or zero, never NaN. Gauss–Jacobi is Golub and Welsch's at every order, `O(n³)` time and `O(n²)` memory, its total weight `2^(α+β+1) B(α+1, β+1)` formed from logarithms where it leaves the range of an `f64`; the logarithms cancel, and cost about `(α + β)/4` ulps of every weight. An `f32` rule is the `f64` rule rounded once.

The outermost weights of Hermite's rule fall below the smallest normal `f64` from 371 points on (`f32`: 52), and Laguerre's from 186 (`f32`: 27). A sum of the rule's terms is then still right for an integrand that does not grow like `e^(x²)` or `eˣ`; for one that does, write the integral without the weight function and use `hermite_scaled` or `laguerre_scaled`, whose weights stay near 1.

```rust
use a::math::numerics::{QuadOptions, hermite, quad, quasi_monte_carlo, tanh_sinh};

// An end singularity, by QAGS and by tanh-sinh: ∫₀¹ x^(−1/2) dx = 2.
let r = quad(|x: f64| 1.0 / x.sqrt(), 0.0, 1.0).unwrap();
assert!(r.converged && (r.value - 2.0).abs() <= r.error);
let t = tanh_sinh(|x: f64| 1.0 / x.sqrt(), 0.0, 1.0, &QuadOptions::new()).unwrap();
assert!(t.converged && (t.value - 2.0).abs() < 1e-14);

// An infinite interval, ∫₁^∞ x^(−1.1) dx = 10, and a divergent one, ∫₁^∞ dx/x, reported.
let r = quad(|x: f64| x.powf(-1.1), 1.0, f64::INFINITY).unwrap();
assert!(r.converged && (r.value - 10.0).abs() <= r.error);
assert!(!quad(|x: f64| 1.0 / x, 1.0, f64::INFINITY).unwrap().converged);

// A bump far out on a half-line, which QUADPACK's running sums lose: ∫₀^∞ e^(−(x−40)²) dx = √π.
let r = quad(|x: f64| (-(x - 40.0) * (x - 40.0)).exp(), 0.0, f64::INFINITY).unwrap();
assert!(r.converged && (r.value - core::f64::consts::PI.sqrt()).abs() <= r.error);

// Gauss–Hermite: ∫ x² e^(−x²) dx = √π/2.
let g = hermite::<f64>(20).integrate(|x: f64| x * x);
assert!((g - core::f64::consts::PI.sqrt() / 2.0).abs() < 1e-14);

// Ten dimensions by quasi-Monte Carlo: ∫ ∏ (xᵢ + ½) over the unit cube is 1.
let f = |x: &[f64]| x.iter().map(|t| t + 0.5).product::<f64>();
let q = quasi_monte_carlo(f, &[0.0; 10], &[1.0; 10], 1 << 14).unwrap();
assert!((q.value - 1.0).abs() < 5.0 * q.error && q.error < 1e-2);
```

**Cubature** (`quad/cubature.rs`): the rule of degree 7 integrates every monomial of degree 7 or less exactly on one box, in 2 to 6 dimensions, and `x₁⁸` not; a polynomial of degree 5 converges on the first box. Genz's oscillatory, product-peak, corner-peak and Gaussian families, 6 random members each in 2 to 6 dimensions at `10⁻⁶` (and, outside the tests, 40 members each at `10⁻⁶` and `10⁻⁹` with budgets up to 200,000 evaluations): no converged result beyond its estimate, where Genz and Malik's `|I₇ − I₅|` alone was 18 to 56 times below the true error of corner peaks in 3 to 5 dimensions. The continuous and the discontinuous family, split at their kinks and their jump, converge to within their estimates. Gaussians of width 0.03 about `(0.7, 0.3)` and `(0.7017, 0.3004)` in the unit square, and about `(0.238, 0.572, 0.788)` in the unit cube, are no longer accepted on their first box (the code of the second review returned `10⁻²⁰` and `10⁻⁴²`, converged). On the second review's sweep of narrow Gaussian peaks — 60 random centres at least six widths inside the unit square or cube, 2000 boxes and 400,000 evaluations — the converged results beyond their estimates at `10⁻⁴`, `10⁻⁶` and `10⁻⁸` fell, with that guard, from 43, 39 and 30 to 2, 3 and 4 for width 0.03 in two dimensions and from 60, 59 and 57 to 6, 8 and 10 for width 0.01, and in three dimensions from 57 and 52 to 2 and 1 for width 0.03 (none converges at `10⁻⁸` within the budget), and from 60 each to 6, 6 and 9 for width 0.01; what is left, and the rates of width 0.003, are in Limits, where they are a limit of the method. A test holds the rates of widths 0.01 and 0.003 as upper bounds, and checks the remedy Limits gives on every one of those 240 peaks at each tolerance: each integrated in `3^d` pieces cut at `c ± 5w` along every axis is converged and within its estimate. The Genz families give the same results as before the guard, to the last bit. `k` boxes cost `(2k − 1)·(2^d + 2d² + 2d + 1)` evaluations; a box of 12 or 15 dimensions is integrated, one of 17 or more refused.

**Monte Carlo** (`quad/monte_carlo.rs`, `data/gen_sobol.py`, `data/gen_xoshiro.py`): the generator's first outputs equal those of the published xoshiro256** and SplitMix64 algorithms; the Sobol' points equal SciPy's (`scipy.stats.qmc.Sobol(d, scramble=False, bits=32)`) in all 1024 dimensions, the first 64 points in full in 1 to 8 dimensions and a checksum of the first 4096 in each, and the first `2^m` values of every coordinate are the multiples of `2^(−m)`. Over 400 seeds of 4096 samples on three integrals (outside the tests, which take 200 seeds of 1024) the true error is within two standard errors 95.5–96.3% of the time and within three 99.5–99.8% for plain Monte Carlo (a normal mean: 95.4% and 99.7%), and 91.2–92.2% and 97.8–98.8% for the shifted Sobol' rule (Student's t with 7 degrees of freedom: 91.4% and 98.0%). With `2^16` samples the quasi-random rule gives `∫ xyz = 1/8` within 3.8·10⁻⁵ (estimate 3.1·10⁻⁵), the simplex volume `1/6` within 5.1·10⁻⁵, `∏ xᵢ` in six dimensions within 5.3·10⁻⁵, and `∏ (xᵢ + ½)` in ten and forty dimensions within 5.1·10⁻⁴ and 1.1·10⁻²; `f32` integrands are summed in `f64` and agree with the `f64` rule to `10⁻⁶`.

**Gaussian rules** (`gauss.rs`, `gauss/reference.rs`, `gauss/data/gen_gauss.py`): against nodes and weights from mpmath at 60 digits (Newton's method on the recurrence, each zero's index proved by a Sturm count, the weights as Christoffel numbers), for 1 to 2000 points (and 5000 and 10,000 in a test for a release build): every node of every rule, and every weight of the Legendre, Hermite and Laguerre rules, is the correctly rounded `f64` and, rounded once, the correctly rounded `f32` — the tests allow no ulp — the scaled weights within 2 ulps, the Jacobi weights for 16 pairs `(α, β)` from `(−0.999, −0.999)` to `(600, 600)` within 3 ulps (31 at `α = β = 600`, through the logarithm of its total weight); weights whose true value is below half the smallest subnormal are zero. The rules integrate the moments of their weight functions exactly to degree `2n − 1` (to `4·10⁻¹⁶` for Legendre up to 128 points, to `10⁻¹³` for the normalised moments of Hermite and Laguerre up to 200), and their weights sum to `2`, `√π` and `1` within 8 ulps up to 2000 points. The Jacobi total weight, against mpmath, for `α = β` (outside the tests but for `10¹²` and `10¹⁵`): 260 ulps off at `10³`, `10⁻⁸` relatively at `10⁸`, `10⁻⁴` at `10¹²`, 11% at `10¹⁵`, and, before the weights were made NaN from `α + β = 2⁵³` on, 79% at `10¹⁶`. `scipy.special.roots_legendre`, `roots_hermite` and `roots_laguerre` agree with the nodes to 14 eps and differ in the weights by up to 1.1·10⁵ eps at 150 points — their own error, as the 60-digit reference shows. The asymptotic first guesses are within `1.4·10⁻³` of the spacing of the zeros from 100 points on (Laguerre's; Hermite's `1.4·10⁻⁴`, Legendre's `10⁻⁸`), falling with the order. The three rules of 20,000 points take under 300 seconds in a release build (a test that is ignored in a debug build; the review measured 12, 10.5 and 23.6 seconds and peak memory of 1 to 3 MB), where the dense eigenproblem would need 3.2 GB for its matrix alone.

### Limits

- Every rule samples the integrand. A feature at which no node looks — every value evaluated exactly 0, or values so far down its tail that they weigh nothing — is not there for the rule, and the result is converged without it (`scipy.integrate.quad` alike). On an infinite interval the first panel of QAGI looks at `[a, ∞)` only at the distances 0.0043, 0.026, 0.073, 0.15, 0.26, 0.42, 0.66, 1, 1.5, 2.4, 3.8, 6.7, 13.8, 38.3 and 233 from `a`: a bump of width 1 about `a + 80`, or of width 0.1 about `a + 20`, is missed so — 25 of the 60 random Gaussian bumps on `[0, ∞)` of the first review, 2 of the 60 on the line. On a finite interval two narrow peaks are the common case: of 400 random pairs of peaks of width `10⁻³` on `[0, 1]`, one in each half, at `10⁻¹²`, 309 of the 397 converged results of `quad` are beyond their estimates, 350 of 383 of `quad_g7k15`, 139 of 400 of `tanh_sinh` and none of `clenshaw_curtis`, whose panels look at 257 points; of width `3·10⁻⁴`, 381 of 395, 386 of 390, 316 of 398 and 261 of 382. In every one of these runs no node saw one of the two peaks above `10⁻³` of its height, mostly not above `10⁻²⁰`. Split the interval at the features where they are known.
- What the evidence of QAGS and `clenshaw_curtis` guarantees, and what it does not. A value that a node of either has seen, and that the nodes of the half it lies in after a bisection do not account for (neither neighbour sees a tenth of it), stays in the error of that half, and of the halves of the half, as its share of the `∫|f|` of the interval that saw it (for the centre of a bisected interval of QAGS, its value times the gap to the half's outermost node), until a node beside it sees a tenth of it; an interval or panel keeps up to four such values, the heaviest. And no rule accepts an estimate whose error is a tenth of it or more, above the round-off of its sums. So no result is accepted while a value so kept, whose share is above the tolerance, lies unaccounted for between the nodes. That is not the promise that a feature some node has seen is found. A value seen far down the tail of a narrow feature is a share far below the feature's own, and nothing in the values tells a tall narrow feature from a small one there. A node that sees a tenth of a value accounts for it, and if the interval about that node is bisected in turn, the node's own value and share, of the smaller interval, are what stands for the feature: the evidence can shrink so by a tenth and more a step, and fall below a loose tolerance before a node finds the feature — a peak of width `10⁻³` about 0.288 that the first bisection saw at 0.35 of its height was accounted for at 0.036 and then at 0.0079 of it, and `quad` returned `1.81·10⁻³ ± 6.4·10⁻⁵` at `10⁻⁴` for `3.00·10⁻³` (SciPy `1.774·10⁻³ ± 3.5·10⁻⁶`). A fifth feature in one interval loses its evidence to the four heaviest, and two nodes can straddle a peak on which the two rules of a pair agree. On the pairs of peaks above, both in `[0.05, 0.45]` or one in each half, widths `10⁻³` and `3·10⁻⁴`, of the converged runs whose nodes saw both peaks above `10⁻³` of their heights none is beyond its estimate at `10⁻¹²` or `10⁻⁸`, for any of the four rules; at `10⁻⁴`, 33 of 246 runs of `quad`, 24 of 142 of `quad_g7k15`, 154 of 1009 of `clenshaw_curtis` and 3 of 658 of `tanh_sinh` are, up to 120, 81, 230 and 3.7 times (the tests hold the first three as upper bounds). Of the converged runs of the second review's 1200 far or narrow features, 37 of 4262 (`quad`) and 27 of 4266 (`quad_g7k15`) are beyond their estimates after a node saw something of the feature — the second of two bumps seen below `10⁻⁶` of its height, or at `10⁻³` below `4·10⁻⁴`, Lorentzians seen below `1.5·10⁻⁶` of theirs, one peak straddled by two nodes (where SciPy sees nothing at all), and 4 at the round-off level — and none of `clenshaw_curtis` or `tanh_sinh`. Where the features are known, split the interval at them; otherwise ask for a tighter tolerance, against which the evidence that is kept weighs more (none of these runs failed so at `10⁻⁸`).
- QAGS's extrapolation is made for singular ends. An integrable singularity inside the interval can lead it astray as it leads QUADPACK astray: of 400 random `|x − c|^p` at `10⁻⁸`, 2 (`quad_g7k15`) and 1 (`quad`) converged results were more than ten times the tolerance off, up to 5500 and 26 times their estimates — SciPy has 2 on the same family. A non-integrable one can be taken for an integrable one: `∫₀¹ dx/|x − 0.2621740234542617|` is converged at `10⁻³` as `37.31 ± 0.031`, as in SciPy. Split the interval at the singular point.
- QUADPACK's test for a divergent integral misfires where an end singularity cancels to an integral of 0: `∫₀¹ (x^(−p) − 1/(1 − p)) dx` comes back right to `10⁻¹⁴` for `p` = 0.6 to 0.8, with an error of 0.37 to 1.8 and not converged (SciPy flags it too).
- An integrand that loses digits in its own argument has an error from the rounding of the points it is evaluated at, about `ε|x|` times `∫|f′|`, which no difference of two estimates on the same points sees. `clenshaw_curtis` and `tanh_sinh` hold it in their estimates (the rounding of a point to `f32` as four standard deviations of independent errors, which is a quarter too small for `clenshaw_curtis` on `(x − 1000)^p` over `[1000, 1007.6]` in `f32`, whose numbers are `6·10⁻⁵` apart there: 3 converged results of the first review's families in `f32`, at most 1.34 times their estimates, `1.5·10⁻⁴` relatively). QAGS does not: a bound of that kind, over the nodes next to a singular end away from 0, would be orders of magnitude above the error there, where QAGS integrates `(1 − x)^(−1/2)` on `[0, 1]` to `1.2·10⁻¹³`. `quad_g7k15` converges on `(x − 1000)^2.2` over `[1000, 1006.12]` at `10⁻¹²` `2.9·10⁻¹²` off with the estimate `1.1·10⁻¹³` (`quad` within its estimate); on `(x − a)^p` over `[a, a + 1.5]` and `(b − x)^p`, 200 exponents `p` in `(−0.9, 0.5)` at `10⁻¹²`, `quad` has 17 of 380 converged runs beyond their estimates at the end 2, 48 of 384 at −6, 66 of 382 at 10 and 76 of 370 at 100, up to 7.0, 7.4, 6.8 and 21.6 times, and at the end 100 also 54 of 400 at every tolerance from `10⁻¹⁰` to `10⁻⁶`, 6 times, the true errors about `10⁻¹²` relatively; `quad_g7k15` 13 and 20 at the ends 2 and −6 (the second review's probe; at the end 1, `p` in `(−0.95, −0.9)`, 5 runs at 2.4 times). Write such an integrand in the distance from the point, on an interval that starts at 0.
- `tanh_sinh` approaches a nonzero end only to within an ulp of it: what a singularity at `x = 1` puts in that last ulp stays in the error estimate, and `(1 − x)^(−1/2)` is not converged at `10⁻¹²` (1.0·10⁻⁸ off, estimate 5.5·10⁻⁷). Write such an integrand in the distance to that end, on an interval that starts at 0, or use `quad`. The same holds for an interval far from 0 against its width: on `[10⁶, 10⁶ + 1]` the ends are approached to `10⁻¹⁰` of the width. Its levels refine everywhere at once, so a narrow feature inside costs evaluations everywhere, and one between all the nodes (every value 0) is not there for the rule; in the second review's sweeps no converged result whose nodes saw a feature is beyond its estimate any more (566 of 3933 were, in `f64`, on the code it reviewed). An inner kink like `|x − c|^p` can still fool it at a loose tolerance: of 60 such integrands at four tolerances, 3 converged results at `10⁻³` in `f64` and 8 at `10⁻³` and `10⁻⁵` in `f32` were beyond their estimates, up to 14 times. A kink, a jump or a peak inside the interval is for `quad`, and `x^(−0.99)` is beyond any rule that samples it: 0.083 of its integral lies below the smallest normal number.
- `clenshaw_curtis` evaluates the integrand at the ends of its interval and takes no infinite limit.
- QAGS evaluates no node at or beyond an end of an interval at least 460 ulps of its larger end wide with the G10–K21 pair in `f64` (234 with G7–K15), and 231 (118) in `f32`: the outermost node of the first panel lies `(1 − u)` of the half-width from the end, `u` its abscissa, and the centre's own rounding takes half an ulp of that room in `f64` (in `f32` the nodes are formed in `f64` and rounded once), so the widths are `2/(1 − u)` and `1/(1 − u)` ulps; a test pins them on widths of 1 to 600 ulps at four ends. On a narrower interval, and on `[a, ∞)` with `|a|` from `2⁴⁶` (`f64`; `2¹⁷` in `f32`) on, the first panel's outermost nodes round onto the ends, or beyond them where the centre itself rounds onto one (`[1, 1 + ε]`), and an integrand infinite or undefined there gives a result that is not a finite number, not converged. QAGS takes an interval wider than the largest finite number, its centre and half-width formed from the halves of its ends (`∫ 10⁻³⁰⁰ dx` over `[−10³⁰⁸, 10³⁰⁸]` is `2·10⁸`, converged); the other rules return NaN for one, not converged.
- `cubature`'s error estimate assumes the integrand smooth on the scale of each box, and no rule on finitely many points can certify a feature narrower than their spacing; every adaptive cubature shares this. A kink or a jump in the band between a box's outermost points (`λ₃ = 0.949` of its half-width) and its faces is not seen by any point, and Genz's continuous and discontinuous families, unsplit, are often converged and wrong — integrate such an integrand by pieces split at the kink or the jump, or by Monte Carlo. In the first review, 316 of 526 converged results on the discontinuous family were beyond their estimates (SciPy's Genz–Malik cubature alike), and 5 of 524 on corner peaks of twice the tested difficulty, by up to 79 times. A narrow peak is wrong three ways. Between all the points of the first boxes it is not there: every value 0, or a far tail (`10⁻²⁵` of its height and less in the sweep below) that the halves of the box, seeing exactly 0, then replace with `0 ± 0`. Its tail beyond a face between two boxes is lost where the box across the face has no point near it: a peak of width 0.01 about `(0.509, 0.565)` has 11% of itself in `[0, 0.5] × [0, 1]`, whose nearest point sees `e^(−46)` of its height — a one-dimensional rule samples the very point it bisects at, a box meets its faces only on a few lines. And a box many widths of the peak long, whose points see it along one line only, overestimates it, its rule spreading the values seen there over the spacing of its points and its null rules, combinations of the same few values, agreeing with it: in the cube a box of 6 × 25 × 6 widths held 80% of a peak of width 0.01 and came out 2.3 times its integral with an error estimate of 1.3%, and the result, twice the integral, was accepted at `10⁻⁴` and `10⁻⁶`, where the absolute tolerance was near or above the integral itself and only the test against a tenth of the estimate was looking. On the second review's sweep (60 random centres at least six widths inside the box, 2000 boxes, 400,000 evaluations), the converged results beyond their estimates at `10⁻⁴`, `10⁻⁶` and `10⁻⁸`:

At the default budget (200 boxes, 100,000 evaluations) the counts are the same or lower, with fewer results converged. A test holds the rates of widths 0.01 and 0.003 as upper bounds. There is no option for a least number of evaluations, and none would certify a narrow feature. Where the features are known, integrate a box a few widths wide about each apart from the rest: with the box cut along every axis at `c ± 5w` and the `3^d` pieces integrated at the same tolerances and summed, none of these 1440 results was beyond its estimate, and at the widths 0.01 and 0.003 every one converged (a test checks these); the pieces of widths 0.1 and 0.03 in the cube at `10⁻⁶` and `10⁻⁸` need more than the budget, as the whole box does. The margin of ten that makes the smooth families honest costs about twice the evaluations, and from 3 dimensions on the default 200 boxes are few for tight tolerances.
- The standard error of `quasi_monte_carlo` is that of `R` estimates that are near normal for a smooth integrand; for one singular in the box they are not: on `x^(−1/3)` over `[0, 1]` the true error exceeded twice the estimate 14–15% of the time and three times it 7% (outside the tests, 1000 seeds), where Student's t predicts 8.6% and 2%. A point of the shifted Sobol' sequence can lie on a face of the box, the lower one or, rounded, the upper (in `f64` about one coordinate in `2^53`, in `f32` one in `2^24`), where an integrand infinite there ends the integration, reported.
- Corners near overflow and underflow: `monte_carlo` of values of `±10³⁰⁸` overflows Welford's update and returns NaN, not converged, where the integral may be 0; a result below the smallest normal number carries an error estimate of at least 50 quanta of the subnormal numbers; spans wider than the largest finite number are as above.
- Gauss–Jacobi has no Newton path of its own: `O(n³)` time and `O(n²)` memory at every order. Its total weight loses about `(α + β)/4` ulps to the cancellation of its logarithms (11% at `α = β = 10¹⁵`), and from `α + β = 2⁵³` on the weights are NaN.

## Differential equations

The initial value problem `y′ = f(t, y)`, `y(t₀) = y₀`, on `f32` and `f64`. The state is a column `Matrix`, and the right-hand side is a closure `f(t, y, dy)` that writes `f(t, y)` into a matrix the solver owns, so a system of any size is written the same way as a scalar equation and the caller allocates nothing. Every method controls its own step size; the entry points are one loop over the trait `OdeMethod`, which a method written outside the crate can implement too.

| Entry point | Returns |
| --- | --- |
| `solve`, `solve_with` | the state at `t₁`, or `None` |
| `integrate`, `integrate_with` | `OdeResult`: the state at `t₁` and the counts of steps, rejections, evaluations, Jacobians and LU decompositions, or `None` |
| `solve_at`, `solve_at_with` | the states at a list of times, read off the continuous extension of each step without keeping the steps, or `None` |
| `solve_ivp`, `solve_ivp_with` | `OdeSolution`: how the integration ended (`OdeStatus`), the `Event`s located on the way, the solution as a function of time (`DenseOutput`) and the counts |

| Method | Order | Error estimate | Dense output | Cost of a step |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `Rk4::fixed(n)` | 4 | none: `n` equal steps | cubic Hermite, order 3 | 4 evaluations |
| `Rk4::new()` | 4 | step doubling with Richardson's estimate | cubic Hermite, order 3 | 11 evaluations a pair of steps |
| `DormandPrince5` | 5 | embedded formula of order 4 | DOPRI5's, order 4, free | 6 evaluations |
| `DormandPrince853` | 8 | embedded formulas of orders 5 and 3 | DOP853's, order 7 | 12 evaluations, 3 more for the dense output |
| `RadauIia` | 5 | RADAU5's: embedded formula of order 3, filtered | the collocation polynomial, stage order 3 | 3 evaluations a Newton iterate, two iterates at least; a real and a complex LU of `n × n` when `h` or `J` changes |
| `Bdf` | 1 to 5, chosen | the next backward difference | the interpolating polynomial of the differences | 1 evaluation a Newton iterate; one LU of `n × n` when `h` or the order changes |

The two stiff methods need the Jacobian `∂f/∂y`: `with_jacobian(jac)` gives it as a closure `jac(t, y, out)` writing the `n × n` matrix (zeroed before the call), and without it they form it by finite differences, `n` evaluations of `f` (and one more for each column whose difference was lost in rounding). Both keep a Jacobian for as long as their Newton iterations converge with it.

`OdeOptions::new()` holds `rtol` (`1e-6`), `atol` (`1e-9`), `h0` (0: chosen by the method), `hmax` (∞), `hmin` (0), `max_steps` (100,000) and `dense_output` (true), set by `with_*` calls. The defaults suit `f64`; in `f32` ask for `1e-4` or so.

**The classical method.** `Rk4::fixed(n)` is Kutta's method of 1901 as the textbooks have it: `n` equal steps, the tolerances not looked at. `Rk4::new()` chooses its steps by step doubling, since the method has no embedded formula: every stretch is covered by one step of `h` and by two of `h/2`, the error of the two half-steps is a fifteenth of their difference from the whole one (Richardson), and the half-steps — classical steps and nothing else, not the extrapolated value — are what is kept. The next pair is `h · min(5, max(0.2, 0.9 · err^(−1/5)))`; `h0`, `hmin` and `hmax` bound the pair, and the pair needs a midpoint, two ulps of `t`. Both forms interpolate each step by the cubic Hermite polynomial through its ends and the slopes there.

**Dormand and Prince's 5(4) pair** (J. R. Dormand, P. J. Prince, *J. Comput. Appl. Math.* 6, 1980) is implemented as in DOPRI5 (E. Hairer, S. P. Nørsett, G. Wanner, *Solving Ordinary Differential Equations I*, 1993, sections II.4–II.6 and IV.2): seven stages, the last of them the derivative at the end of the step and so the first of the next (six new evaluations a step), the solution of order 5 propagated (local extrapolation), the error estimated by the embedded formula of order 4, Gustafsson's PI controller with DOPRI5's constants (`h_new = h · min(10, max(0.2, 0.9 · err^(−0.17) · err_old^(0.04)))`, no growth after a rejection), the first step by Hairer's `hinit`, and DOPRI5's continuous extension of order 4, which costs nothing.

**The 8(5,3) method** (P. J. Prince, J. R. Dormand, *J. Comput. Appl. Math.* 7, 1981) is implemented as in Hairer's DOP853: twelve stages and the derivative at the end of an accepted step; the two embedded estimates combined as `err = |h| · err₅² / √(err₅² + 0.01 · err₃²)`, where `err₅` and `err₃` are the differences of the solution of order 8 from those of orders 5 and 3 divided by `h` (the sums of stages DOP853 forms them from), so that `err` behaves as `h⁸`; the step `h · min(6, max(0.333, 0.9 · err^(−1/8)))`; and the continuous extension of order 7, whose three extra stages are evaluated only for the steps a dense output, an event or an output time needs. The coefficients are DOP853's 30-digit decimals; the tests prove them, and those of the other two methods, by the order conditions on every rooted tree up to the order claimed — and their failure one order up — for the weights, the embedded formulas and the continuous extensions.

**The Radau IIA method.** `RadauIia` is the three-stage Radau IIA collocation method of order 5, L-stable, implemented as in Hairer and Wanner's RADAU5 (E. Hairer, G. Wanner, *Solving Ordinary Differential Equations II*, 2nd edition, 1996, sections IV.5 and IV.8). Its stages solve `Z = h·(A ⊗ I)·F(Z)` by the simplified Newton iteration on `(h⁻¹A⁻¹ ⊗ I − I ⊗ J)·ΔZ = −h⁻¹(A⁻¹ ⊗ I)·Z + F(Z)`; in the coordinates `W = (T⁻¹ ⊗ I)·Z` in which `A⁻¹` is block-diagonal — one real eigenvalue `γ = 3 + 3^(2/3) − 3^(1/3)` and a pair `α ± iβ` — the `3n × 3n` system falls apart into one real system with the matrix `γ/h·I − J` and one complex system with `(α + iβ)/h·I − J`, each solved by `Matrix::lu` (on `Matrix<T>` and on `Matrix<Complex<T>>`), the decompositions kept while `h` and `J` are. The iteration starts from the collocation polynomial of the previous step extrapolated to the new stages; its rate `θ` is watched from the second iterate on, an iteration that diverges (`θ ≥ 0.99`) or would not converge within seven iterates is abandoned and the step retried smaller, and it stops when its error, estimated from a rate measured in the step itself, is below `κ = max(10·ε/rtol, min(0.03, √rtol))` of the tolerances — so a step takes two iterates at least, unless a correction is already below the rounding of the state (`10·ε` times the state in the norm of the tolerances), which no further iterate could improve on. The error is RADAU5's: the difference from an embedded formula of order 3, `γ⁻¹·(h·f(t, y) + Σ ddⱼ·Zⱼ)` with `dd = (−(13 + 7√6)/3, (−13 + 7√6)/3, −1/3)`, filtered through `(I − h·J/γ)⁻¹`, and for the first step and after a rejection filtered a second time through `f(t, y + err)` when it is above one. The step is chosen by Gustafsson's predictive controller with RADAU5's constants: the smaller of `h·fac·err^(−1/4)` (`fac` lowered from 0.9 when the iteration needed many iterates) and `h·0.9·(h/h_old)·(err_old/err²)^(1/4)`, each held to `[h/5, 8h]`, no growth after a rejection, a rejected first step retried at `h/10`, and a step within `[1, 1.2]` of the last one not changed at all when the Jacobian served well, so that the decompositions are kept. The Jacobian is formed again only after a step whose iteration converged slowly (`θ > 0.001`) or that failed with an old Jacobian. The dense output is the collocation polynomial of each step. The constants — the abscissae, the eigenvalues, the transformation `T` (its columns scaled to end in 1, 1 and 0; any scaling serves) and the weights `dd` — are derived exactly in `lib/tests/math/numerics/ode/data/gen_radau.py` (sympy; `dd` from the order-3 conditions of the embedded formula), and the tests check the method they make up against its stability function, its collocation polynomial and its error estimate computed independently. Three things differ from RADAU5: the tolerances are the module's, where RADAU5 first replaces `rtol` by `0.1·rtol^(2/3)` (SciPy's `Radau` does not either); the first step, when not given, is `hinit`'s for an estimate of order 4, where RADAU5 starts from `10⁻⁶`; and the first Newton iterate is never accepted on the rate of the step before. RADAU5 takes `η₀ = max(η_old, ε)^0.8` for it, which after a step that converged fast lets a first correction of up to hundreds of tolerances pass as converged while the Jacobian, kept from earlier steps, no longer fits: where stiff components follow slow ones through a nonlinear coupling, as in Kaps's problem, that leaves the stages far from converged, and the error estimate, computed from the same stages, does not see it (see *Measured*). Requiring a rate of the step's own, as SciPy's `Radau` does, costs one more iterate on the steps RADAU5 would end after one.

**The backward differentiation formulas.** `Bdf` is the variable-order (1 to 5), variable-step method of MATLAB's `ode15s` and SciPy's `BDF` (L. F. Shampine, M. W. Reichelt, "The MATLAB ODE Suite", *SIAM J. Sci. Comput.* 18, 1997; G. D. Byrne, A. C. Hindmarsh, *ACM TOMS* 1, 1975). By default the formulas are `ode15s`'s numerical differentiation formulas, the BDFs with an extra term `−κ·γₖ·(yₙ₊₁ − yₙ₊₁⁽⁰⁾)`, `κ = −0.1850, −1/9, −0.0823, −0.0415, 0` for the orders 1 to 5 (Shampine and Reichelt's choice: error constants `κγₖ + 1/(k + 1)` 37%, 50%, 60% and 43% smaller than the BDFs' at orders 1 to 4, which allows a step 26% longer at the same error at orders 1 to 3 and 12% at order 4); `with_ndf(false)` gives the BDFs themselves, `κ = 0`, and `with_max_order(k)` caps the order (`ode15s`'s `MaxOrder`), since the formulas above order 2 are not A-stable: they are stable on a sector `|arg(−hλ)| < α` with `α` = 86°, 73° and 51° for the BDFs of orders 3 to 5 and 80°, 66° and 51° for the NDFs (Shampine and Reichelt's Table 1; the boundary locus gives 86.03°, 73.35° and 51.84°, and 80.42°, 66.18° and 51.84°). The solution is kept as the backward differences `∇ʲyₙ` of the interpolating polynomial at equally spaced points; the prediction is that polynomial at `tₙ + h`, the corrector is the modified Newton iteration on `I − h/((1 − κ)γₖ)·J` through `Matrix::lu`, at most four iterates, and the error is `(κγₖ + 1/(k + 1))·∇ᵏ⁺¹yₙ₊₁`, read off the correction. A rejected step is retried at `max(0.2, 0.9·err^(−1/(k+1)))` times its size, the safety factor lowered for an iteration that needed many iterates; a change of the step respaces the differences by interpolation (`D ← (R·U)ᵀ·D`, Shampine and Reichelt's matrices), so the formulas are always those of constant steps. After `k + 1` steps of one size the errors of orders `k − 1` and `k + 1` are estimated from `∇ᵏyₙ₊₁` and `∇ᵏ⁺²yₙ₊₁`, and the order and step that allow the longest step are taken (at most ten times longer); until then the step stays, and with it the decomposition. The Jacobian is formed at the start and again only when the iteration fails with an old one, at the predicted point. The dense output is the interpolating polynomial of the differences, of the degree of the order. The integration starts at order 1 from `hinit`'s step for an error of order 2.

**The Jacobian by differences.** Both stiff methods difference `f` as MATLAB's `numjac` does (Shampine and Reichelt, section 5, after D. E. Salane, Sandia report SAND86-1319, 1986): column `j` steps `yⱼ` by `σⱼ·max(|yⱼ|, atol)` in the direction of `fⱼ`, from a factor `σⱼ` that starts at `√ε` and adapts from one Jacobian to the next — the column is evaluated again with ten times the step when its largest change is lost in the rounding of `f`, and `σⱼ` is raised or lowered tenfold when the change is below `ε^(3/4)` or above `ε^(1/4)` of `f`. A fixed increment does not serve every scaling: RADAU5's `√(ε·max(10⁻⁵, |yⱼ|))` steps Robertson's `y₂ ≈ 10⁻¹³` by `5·10⁻¹¹`, which makes `∂f₃/∂y₂` three hundred times too large late in the integration (see *Measured*).

**Step-size control.** A step is accepted when its error estimate, in the root mean square norm of Hairer's codes `√((1/n) Σ (errᵢ / (atol + rtol · max(|yᵢ|, |y₁ᵢ|)))²)`, is at most one. No step is longer than `hmax`, and none but the last is shorter than the floor — `hmin`, and never less than ten ulps of `t` — both to the rounding of the time (for `Rk4::new()` they bound its pairs): a proposal outside is cut or raised, as LSODE raises one to its `hmin`. A rejected step is retried shorter, at `hmin` itself before anything below it, and when the retry would be below the floor the integration ends with `StepTooSmall` rather than looping. The last step, what is left of the interval, is taken whatever its size, so an interval shorter than the floor is tried as one step, and a method that rejects it ends with `StepTooSmall`; tolerances below the rounding of the state (`ε · ‖y‖ > 1` in that norm, the test of LSODE) end it with `ToleranceTooSmall`; a right-hand side that is not finite at a stage, or a Newton iteration that does not converge, rejects the step like a large error. The last step ends on `t₁` exactly — a step that would end within a hundredth of its length of `t₁` is stretched or cut to end there, as in Hairer's codes, but never beyond `hmax` — `t₁ < t₀` integrates backwards, and `t₁ = t₀` returns the initial state.

**Dense output.** Each method writes the continuous extension of its last step as a `DenseStep`, a polynomial in `θ = (t − t₀)/h` in Newton form on nodes of the method's choosing (`0, 1, 0, 1, …` for Hairer's nested forms, `0, 0, 1` for the Hermite cubic, `1, c₂, c₁` for the collocation polynomial, `1, 0, −1, …` for the backward differences). `DenseOutput` keeps them and evaluates `y(t)` anywhere in the integrated interval by bisection on the steps.

**Events.** An `Event` is a function `g(t, y)` with a direction (`Rising`, `Falling`, `Either`, along the direction of the integration) and a terminal flag. After each accepted step the signs of `g` at its ends are compared; a change is located on the continuous extension by the crate's `brent`, finished by bisection to four ulps of the time, so the event time is as accurate as the dense output and costs no evaluation of `f`. Events in one step are reported in the order of their times, and the first terminal one ends the integration there. Leaving zero is not an event, so a zero at `t₀` is not reported; the sign a function leaves zero with is read just after the start of the step (CVODE's rule), so a crossing later in that step is still found. A function that touches zero without crossing is not an event, unless a step ends on its zero exactly, which is reported once. Two crossings inside one step cancel: limit the step with `hmax` where that matters.

A failed integration is never passed off as the solution at `t₁`: `solve`, `integrate` and `solve_at` return `None`, and `solve_ivp` returns `OdeStatus::Failed` with the reason (`InvalidInput`, `NonFinite`, `StepTooSmall`, `ToleranceTooSmall`, `MaxSteps`, `EventNotFinite`) and the last point it reached.

```rust
use a::math::matrix::Matrix;
use a::math::numerics::{
    Bdf, DormandPrince853, Event, EventDirection, OdeOptions, OdeStatus, RadauIia,
    integrate_with, solve_at, solve_ivp_with,
};

// A body thrown up at 5 m/s from a height of 2 m, under g = 9.81: when does it land?
let f = |_t: f64, y: &Matrix<f64>, dy: &mut Matrix<f64>| {
    dy[(0, 0)] = y[(1, 0)];
    dy[(1, 0)] = -9.81;
};
let ground = Event::new(|_t: f64, y: &Matrix<f64>| y[(0, 0)])
    .with_direction(EventDirection::Falling)
    .with_terminal(true);
let y0 = Matrix::from_rows(&[[2.0], [5.0]]);
let opts = OdeOptions::new().with_tolerances(1e-10, 1e-12);
let s = solve_ivp_with(f, &y0, 0.0, 10.0, DormandPrince853::new(), &mut [ground], &opts);
assert_eq!(s.status, OdeStatus::Terminated);
let landing = (5.0 + (25.0f64 + 2.0 * 9.81 * 2.0).sqrt()) / 9.81;
assert!((s.t - landing).abs() < 1e-12);
// The solution between, from the dense output: the top of the flight at t = 5/9.81.
let top = s.eval(5.0 / 9.81).unwrap();
assert!((top[(0, 0)] - (2.0 + 25.0 / (2.0 * 9.81))).abs() < 1e-12);

// The states at given times only, without keeping the steps.
let decay = |_t: f64, y: &Matrix<f64>, dy: &mut Matrix<f64>| dy[(0, 0)] = -y[(0, 0)];
let ts = [0.5, 1.0, 2.0];
let ys = solve_at(decay, &Matrix::from_rows(&[[1.0]]), 0.0, &ts, DormandPrince853::new()).unwrap();
for (t, y) in ts.iter().zip(&ys) {
    assert!((y[(0, 0)] - (-t).exp()).abs() < 1e-7);
}

// Robertson's chemical kinetics over its whole range, to t = 10¹¹.
let f = |_t: f64, y: &Matrix<f64>, dy: &mut Matrix<f64>| {
    let (a, b, c) = (y[(0, 0)], y[(1, 0)], y[(2, 0)]);
    dy[(0, 0)] = -0.04 * a + 1e4 * b * c;
    dy[(1, 0)] = 0.04 * a - 1e4 * b * c - 3e7 * b * b;
    dy[(2, 0)] = 3e7 * b * b;
};
// Its Jacobian, for Radau; the BDFs difference f instead.
let jac = |_t: f64, y: &Matrix<f64>, j: &mut Matrix<f64>| {
    let (b, c) = (y[(1, 0)], y[(2, 0)]);
    j[(0, 0)] = -0.04;
    j[(0, 1)] = 1e4 * c;
    j[(0, 2)] = 1e4 * b;
    j[(1, 0)] = 0.04;
    j[(1, 1)] = -1e4 * c - 6e7 * b;
    j[(1, 2)] = -1e4 * b;
    j[(2, 1)] = 6e7 * b;
};
let y0 = Matrix::from_rows(&[[1.0], [0.0], [0.0]]);
let opts = OdeOptions::new().with_tolerances(1e-6, 1e-14);
let radau = integrate_with(f, &y0, 0.0, 1e11, RadauIia::new().with_jacobian(jac), &opts).unwrap();
let bdf = integrate_with(f, &y0, 0.0, 1e11, Bdf::new(), &opts).unwrap();
// The values of the Test Set for IVP Solvers, y₁ = 2.08334·10⁻⁸ and y₂ = 8.33336·10⁻¹⁴.
for r in [&radau, &bdf] {
    assert!((r.y[(0, 0)] - 2.083_340_15e-8).abs() < 1e-12);
    assert!((r.y[(1, 0)] - 8.333_360_8e-14).abs() < 1e-17);
}
// Twenty-two decades of time in a few hundred steps; the BDFs keep a Jacobian for many.
assert!(radau.steps < 600 && bdf.steps < 1000 && bdf.njev < 30);
```

### Measured

The six non-stiff problems are exponential decay on `[0, 10]`, the harmonic oscillator over ten periods, Kepler's problem at eccentricity 0.9 over three periods, Lotka–Volterra on `[0, 15]`, Arenstorf's orbit over one period and the Brusselator on `[0, 20]`, at `rtol = atol`; errors are the largest of `|yᵢ − exactᵢ| / (1 + |exactᵢ|)` at `t₁`. *The codes reproduced.* `DormandPrince5` takes exactly the steps of Hairer's DOPRI5 as SciPy bundles it (`scipy.integrate.ode("dopri5")`): the same accepted steps, rejections and evaluations in all 18 runs (six problems at `1e-4`, `1e-7`, `1e-10`), the final states agreeing to `5·10⁻⁵` of the error they share. `DormandPrince853` takes exactly the steps of a literal transcription of DOP853's step loop in all 24 runs (to `1e-13`), agreeing to 7% of the shared error (Kepler, where rounding is amplified most). ### Limits

The explicit methods do not detect stiffness as DOPRI5 and DOP853 do: a stiff problem makes them take the small steps stability needs until `max_steps`. The tolerances are scalars, not one per component, so an `atol` far above the size of a component leaves that component uncontrolled: Robertson's problem to `10¹¹` at SciPy's defaults `rtol = 1e-3`, `atol = 1e-6`, with `y₂` near `10⁻¹⁴`, ends with `Bdf` at `y₁ = −4·10⁷`, `y₃ = 4·10⁷`, and over 120 such loose settings (`rtol` from `5e-4` to `1e-2`, `atol` from `1e-7` to `1e-5`) the stiff methods end in a state of that kind in 49 of 120 runs (19 of them `RadauIia`'s), SciPy's `Radau` and `BDF` in 35 (17 `Radau`'s) and fail in 2 more; give an `atol` below the components that matter. Two sign changes of an event function inside one step cancel and go unnoticed: over random linear systems that is 12 of 83 checkable crossings with `DormandPrince853` at `1e-3`, whose steps are the longest, and every crossing missed by any method was one of such a pair; limit the step with `hmax` below the shortest time between two crossings where each one matters. The classical method's adaptive form costs eleven evaluations a pair of steps and its continuous extension is only of the third order; its tolerance bounds the error of each step, so that at stringent tolerances its global error is many tolerances — at `1e-9` (`rtol = atol`) 96 on Lotka–Volterra and 3.6 on the Brusselator, against 6.2 and 1.1 for `DormandPrince5`, and between the steps up to 330 and 120. With `atol = 0` the Newton iteration of a stiff method has nothing to measure a component against where it is exactly zero — at the start of the step for `RadauIia`, in the prediction for `Bdf` — and cannot converge: at `rtol = 1e-6` the oscillator from `(1, 0)` ends with `StepTooSmall` at `t₀` for `RadauIia`, and `y′ = t` from 0 with `MaxSteps` near `t = 0` for both, where the explicit methods finish; give a positive `atol`. An interval shorter than the floor is one step, which a stiff method rejects where its estimate for so long a step is large: on `y′ = −y` over `[10¹⁵, 10¹⁵ + 0.25]` (the floor there, `10·ε·|t|`, is 2.2) `RadauIia` and `Bdf` end with `StepTooSmall` where the explicit methods take the step. Such a step is accepted on the method's own estimate like any other, which for so long a step can be far off: `Rk4::new()` on `y′ = cos(t)·y` over `[−10¹⁵, −10¹⁵ + 1]` (8 ulps of `t`) finishes in one pair of steps 2.1·10⁻⁵ off, some 21 tolerances at `rtol = 1e-6`, where `DormandPrince5` rejects the step and ends with `StepTooSmall`. The dense output keeps `n·(k + 1)` numbers a step (`k + 1` = 8 for the 8(5,3) method). The stiff methods solve `y′ = f(t, y)` only — no mass matrix, no differential-algebraic systems — and their linear algebra is dense: each decomposition costs `O(n³)`, a differenced Jacobian `n` evaluations, and nothing exploits a sparse or banded Jacobian. `RadauIia`'s dense output is of the stage order 3, so between the long steps it takes on stiff problems it is less accurate than at them (see above); limit the step with `hmax` where the solution between the steps matters. `RadauIia` re-forms the Jacobian and the decompositions more often than SciPy's `Radau` (RADAU5's rules, which suit small systems with cheap Jacobians), and takes one more Newton iterate than RADAU5 on the steps whose first iterate RADAU5 accepts on the rate of the step before. In `f32` a stiff problem is solved only as far as the rounding of `f` allows: where `ε·‖J‖·‖y‖` approaches the tolerance the Newton iteration cannot meet its own, and over random linear systems with eigenvalues down to `−10⁶` at `rtol = 1e-4`, `atol = 1e-7`, up to 15% of the runs of a method end with `StepTooSmall` and the others within 140 tolerances (`RadauIia`, on a system with eigenvalues near the imaginary axis) and 100 (`Bdf`), and at `1e-3` within 14 and 45; such problems want `f64`. `Bdf`'s formulas of orders 3 to 5 are not A-stable: a problem with eigenvalues close to the imaginary axis may need `with_max_order(2)`. The step-size control of `Bdf`, by the module's `max(|yₙ|, |yₙ₊₁|)` scale, is a little looser than SciPy's where a component decays fast.

## Optimisation

| Function | Needs | Method | `converged` means |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `golden_section` | a bracket, unimodality | the golden section | the bracket is within the tolerance on the point |
| `minimize` | a bracket, unimodality | Brent's parabolic interpolation and golden section | the same |
| `nelder_mead` | `f`, a start and a first step | the simplex of Nelder and Mead | the simplex has closed and no point a tolerance away along an axis is better |
| `conjugate_gradient` | `f` and ∇`f` | Polak–Ribière with restarts | `‖∇f‖∞ ≤ gtol` |
| `bfgs` | `f` and ∇`f` | the inverse BFGS update | `‖∇f‖∞ ≤ gtol` |
| `lbfgsb` | `f`, ∇`f` and bounds `l ≤ x ≤ u` | L-BFGS-B | the projected gradient is at most `pgtol`, or a step lowered `f` by no more than `ftol·max(\|f\|, 1)` |
| `gauss_newton` | the residual and its Jacobian | the Gauss–Newton step by QR | the fit is stationary, or exact to rounding, by one of four tests |
| `levenberg_marquardt` | the residual and its Jacobian | Moré's trust-region Levenberg–Marquardt, by QR | the same |

Each has a `_with` form that takes the options: `OptOptions` (`xtol`, `rtol`, `ftol`, `gtol`, `max_iter` — which method reads which field is in its documentation) for all but `lbfgsb`, whose `LbfgsbOptions` holds the memory size, `pgtol`, `ftol` and the limits on iterations and evaluations.

**One variable.** `golden_section` keeps the part of the bracket that holds the lower of two points at 0.382 and 0.618 of it. Each new point is placed 0.382 of the way from the point kept to the near end rather than computed from the ends, because from the ends the rounding of one step moves the pair off the golden ratio by 1.6 times as much again in the next, and a long search loses it. `minimize` is `localmin` of Brent's *Algorithms for Minimization without Derivatives* (1973, chapter 5): the minimum of the parabola through the three best points when it falls inside the bracket and is shorter than half the step before last, a golden-section step otherwise. Both stop once the bracket is no wider than `xtol + rtol·max(|x|, 1)` about the best point — `6·10⁻⁸` relative with the default `rtol = 4√ε` in `f64` — and `minimize` never evaluates `f` at two points closer than a quarter of that. A value that is not finite counts as `+∞`, so a function defined on part of the bracket is minimised over that part. A bracket whose width `b − a` is not a float is refused.

**Nelder and Mead.** The moves are those of Nelder and Mead (1965) as Lagarias, Reeds, Wright and Wright (1998) set them out: reflection, expansion to twice as far, contraction half way — outside the simplex when the reflected point beats the worst corner, inside it when it does not — and a shrink by half towards the best corner. A simplex can close on a point that is no minimum, by flattening itself against a slope or by starting smaller than the tolerance; before it says `converged` the method therefore polls the `2n` points a tolerance away from its best corner along the axes, and goes on from a new simplex if one of them is better.

**Gradient methods.** Both find their steps by the line search of Moré and Thuente (*Line search algorithms with guaranteed sufficient decrease*, ACM TOMS 20, 1994): a safeguarded cubic or quadratic interpolation in an interval that is lengthened until it brackets a point satisfying the strong Wolfe conditions `φ(α) ≤ φ(0) + μαφ′(0)`, `|φ′(α)| ≤ η|φ′(0)|`, and then narrowed, with a bisection whenever two trials have not shrunk it to 0.66 of itself, and on the auxiliary function `ψ` until a point with a non-negative slope has been seen. `conjugate_gradient` uses `η = 0.1` and Polak and Ribière's `β` held at zero or above, restarting down the gradient every `n` steps and after a step that did not meet the curvature condition. Its first trial step is the one that would repeat the last decrease, `2(f − f_before)/φ′(0)` (Nocedal and Wright, 3.60); after a step that gained much where the gradient is now small that guess can be enormous, and a search down the gradient that began from it and found nothing is tried once more from the step of unit length. `bfgs` uses `η = 0.9` and the update `H ← (I − ρsyᵀ)H(I − ρysᵀ) + ρssᵀ` multiplied out in `O(n²)`, starting from the identity as SciPy does (scaling it by `yᵀs/yᵀy` after the first step, as Nocedal and Wright suggest, was measured to cost three to nine times the iterations on badly conditioned quadratics). Both stop on `‖∇f‖∞ ≤ gtol` and on nothing else: a short step or a small decrease is what a stalled method shows as well as a finished one. A step is one to a lower value of `f` or, where the change of `f` along it is lost in the rounding of `f`, a step of the strong Wolfe conditions that lowers `‖∇f‖∞`. At the limit of the arithmetic the decrease condition holds in rounding for a step that does not move the point at all; that is not a step, and a direction along which the search finds none is tried again down the gradient, after which the method stops, with `converged` false unless the gradient test holds.

**L-BFGS-B.** `lbfgsb` is the method of Byrd, Lu, Nocedal and Zhu (*A limited memory algorithm for bound constrained optimization*, SIAM J. Sci. Comput. 16, 1995) in the form of the reference code of Zhu, Byrd, Lu and Nocedal (ACM TOMS 23, 1997), with the subspace step of Morales and Nocedal (ACM TOMS 38, 2011). The Hessian is approximated by the limited-memory BFGS matrix of the last `m` pairs (10 by default) in the compact form `B = θI − W·M·Wᵀ`, `W = [Y, θS]`, nothing of size `n × n` ever formed. Each iteration finds the generalised Cauchy point, the first minimiser of the quadratic model along the projected steepest-descent path, taking the breakpoints in order from a heap and updating the slope and curvature of the model on each piece in `O(m²)`; minimises the model over the variables that are free there by the direct primal method, the reduced matrix inverted through the `2m × 2m` matrix `K` factored as `L·E·Lᵀ` from two Cholesky factorisations; projects that minimiser onto the box, or cuts it back to the box when the projection no longer leads downhill; and searches along the line to it by the Moré–Thuente search with the constants of the reference code, `μ = 10⁻³`, `η = 0.9`, the step held to the box and at most 20 evaluations. A pair with `sᵀy ≤ ε·(−∇fᵀs)` is not stored. The start is projected into the box; a bound of `±∞` is no bound, `l = u` fixes a variable, and `l > u` anywhere gives `None`. It stops on the two tests of the reference code: the projected gradient `‖P(x − ∇f) − x‖∞ ≤ pgtol` (`10⁻⁵`), or a step that lowered `f` by no more than `ftol·max(|f_old|, |f|, 1)` (`ftol = 10⁷·ε` for `f64`, SciPy's default, and `10·ε` for `f32`). Which of the two it was is in `LbfgsbResult::stop`, with the projected gradient and the active set of the point. A step is progress when it lowers `f`, or, where the change of `f` along it is lost in the rounding of `f`, when it satisfies the strong Wolfe conditions and lowers the projected gradient; such a level step never passes the reduction test, which the reference code lets it do. A line search that finds no progress discards the memory, as the reference code does, and a second failure in a row ends the method with `converged` false.

**Least squares.** Both fits minimise `½‖r(x)‖²` with the linear algebra on `Matrix::qr`. `gauss_newton` takes the whole Gauss–Newton step, the least-squares solution of `J·p = −r`, and returns `None` where the columns of `J` are dependent at the start (a diagonal element of `R` at or below `ε·max(m, n)` times its column); where they become so at a point its steps have led to, it returns that point with `converged` false. `levenberg_marquardt` is Moré's algorithm (*The Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm: implementation and theory*, 1978), that of MINPACK's `lmder`: the step minimises `‖Jp + r‖² + λ‖Dp‖²`, `D` the largest length each column of `J` has had, solved as the least-squares problem `[R; √λ·D]·p = [−Qᵀr; 0]` by QR (never by the normal equations, which square the condition number); `λ` is zero when the Gauss–Newton step lies inside the trust region `‖Dp‖ ≤ Δ`, and otherwise the root of `‖Dp(λ)‖ = Δ` by Moré's safeguarded Newton iteration; `Δ` follows the ratio of the actual to the predicted reduction (shrunk when it is ¼ or less, doubled from the step when it is ¾ or more, the step taken when it is at least `10⁻⁴`). Four tests make `converged` true. At any point: the cosine between the residual and every column of `J` at most `gtol`; or the residual no larger than `2(1 + √n)·ε·‖D_J·x‖`, `D_J` the lengths the columns have there — rounding each parameter to a float moves the model by up to `ε/2·|xₖ|·‖Jₖ‖`, so the exact minimiser of a fit that is exact has a residual of that order, and a point whose residual is no larger cannot be told from it, where the model is linear over the rounding of the parameters; it takes one more step, which solves the linearised problem and can only refine it, and the method stops if the Jacobian holds over that step (below); where no such step can be made — the budget is spent, the damping fails, Gauss–Newton meets dependent columns — the point is returned with `converged` false. On a step that solves the linearised problem: the step of every parameter at most `xtol + rtol·|xⱼ|`, the Jacobian holding over it; or an actual and a predicted reduction both at most `ftol + rtol·cost`. A step solves the linearised problem when `λ` is zero, or when `λ` is at its floor and leaves the linearised residual `r + J·p` no cosine above `ε^¼` against a column (with `D` the current lengths the floor leaves at most half that, so a step that leaves more was held back by a length its column no longer has). The step is judged parameter by parameter because in one norm over all of them a parameter whose column is long, or once was, hides a step that changes another one wholly: measured that way, both fits stopped "converged" at costs of up to `10⁶⁶` from starts where the rate of a decay had the wrong sign, as MINPACK and SciPy's TRF still do. A damped step proves nothing and is never taken for convergence, and neither is a point where a column of `J` that had a length has lost it, a parameter run off to where the model no longer depends on it. The Jacobian holds over a step when, evaluated at the end of the step, it is the one the step was made with to within a quarter, the change of each column weighted by its parameter as in the residual test. The step test and the residual test read the linearised problem as the problem, which beside a pole of the model it is not: for `a·t/(b + t) + c` with `b` near `−tₖ` the Gauss–Newton step moves `b` by about its distance from the pole, a step short against `b`, and within an ulp of the pole the residual, huge, is below the resolution of the residual test as well, since rounding `b` moves the model as much; yet each such step doubles the distance, quarters the cost and cuts the column of `b` to a quarter. Both tests took such points for fits, as MINPACK still does; with the check such a point is not taken for a fit: the fits go on, and either leave the pole or, from a start within an ulp of it, stop with `converged` false. It costs one evaluation of the Jacobian, at the point where the method stops.

```rust
use a::math::matrix::Matrix;
use a::math::numerics::{Bound, LbfgsbStop, OptOptions, lbfgsb, levenberg_marquardt, minimize};

// Brent's method: the minimum of e^x − 2x on [0, 2] is ln 2, found in eleven evaluations.
let r = minimize(|x: f64| x.exp() - 2.0 * x, 0.0, 2.0).unwrap();
assert!(r.converged && (r.x - core::f64::consts::LN_2).abs() <= OptOptions::<f64>::new().tol_at(r.x));
assert_eq!(r.evaluations, 11);

// L-BFGS-B: Rosenbrock's valley with the first variable held to at most ½. The minimum moves to
// (½, ¼), on that bound, and the result says so.
let f = |x: &Matrix<f64>| (1.0 - x[(0, 0)]).powi(2) + 100.0 * (x[(1, 0)] - x[(0, 0)].powi(2)).powi(2);
let g = |x: &Matrix<f64>| {
    let (a, b) = (x[(0, 0)], x[(1, 0)]);
    Matrix::from_column_major(2, 1, vec![-2.0 * (1.0 - a) - 400.0 * a * (b - a * a), 200.0 * (b - a * a)])
};
let lower = Matrix::from_column_major(2, 1, vec![-2.0, -2.0]);
let upper = Matrix::from_column_major(2, 1, vec![0.5, 2.0]);
let r = lbfgsb(f, g, &Matrix::from_column_major(2, 1, vec![-1.2, 1.0]), &lower, &upper).unwrap();
assert_eq!(r.stop, LbfgsbStop::ProjectedGradient);
assert_eq!(r.active, vec![Bound::Upper, Bound::Free]);
assert!(r.x[(0, 0)] == 0.5 && (r.x[(1, 0)] - 0.25).abs() < 1e-9);

// Levenberg and Marquardt: a·e^(−λt) through five exact points of a = 2, λ = 1.5, from a start
// far out.
let t = [0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0];
let residual = |p: &Matrix<f64>| {
    Matrix::from_fn(5, 1, |i, _| p[(0, 0)] * (-p[(1, 0)] * t[i]).exp() - 2.0 * (-1.5 * t[i]).exp())
};
let jacobian = |p: &Matrix<f64>| {
    Matrix::from_fn(5, 2, |i, k| match k {
        0 => (-p[(1, 0)] * t[i]).exp(),
        _ => -p[(0, 0)] * t[i] * (-p[(1, 0)] * t[i]).exp(),
    })
};
let fit = levenberg_marquardt(residual, jacobian, &Matrix::from_column_major(2, 1, vec![0.01, 1.0])).unwrap();
assert!(fit.converged && (fit.x[(0, 0)] - 2.0).abs() < 1e-8 && (fit.x[(1, 0)] - 1.5).abs() < 1e-8);
```

### Measured

References: SciPy 1.18 (`minimize_scalar(method="bounded")`, which is Brent's `fminbound`; `minimize` with BFGS, CG, Nelder-Mead and L-BFGS-B; `least_squares(method="lm")`, which is MINPACK), the certified values of the NIST StRD nonlinear regression problems, and mpmath at 50 digits. The scripts are `lib/tests/math/numerics/data/gen_opt.py` and `gen_lbfgsb.py`; the tests that compare with their output are `cargo test --test math -- numerics::opt:: numerics::lbfgsb::`.

On eleven functions of one variable (parabolas, a quartic with a flat minimum, a kink, `x ln x`, the sine, `cosh`, `x⁶`), `minimize` takes exactly as many evaluations as `fminbound` at the same tolerance — 6 on a parabola, 11 to 13 on smooth functions, 17 on the kink, 24 on `x⁶` — and ends within the tolerance of its point, on six of the eleven at the same float. `golden_section` takes 36 to 43 on the same. `(x − 3)²` in `f32` on `[−10⁴, 10⁴]`, which came back as 7.33 with `converged` true before, is found within `tol_at(3)`; a bracket of `2·10³⁰⁰` closes about zero in some 1,470 golden steps, its two points still in the golden ratio.

### Limits

The bracketed methods need `f` to fall and then rise in its computed values: where they are equal over a stretch of the bracket (`atan(x)²` beyond `|x| = 10¹⁶`) the search can close on that stretch. The simplex's tolerances are relative to the largest coordinate, so on a badly scaled function it closes long before a small coordinate is accurate: on Brown's function it stops with `|∇f|∞ = 434`, as SciPy's does. Gauss–Newton has no damping and no line search: from a start where its whole step overshoots it stops at once, with `converged` false. The conjugate gradient may need many iterations on an ill-conditioned function, 1,576 on a quadratic of condition `10⁶` in ten variables, beyond the default budget of 1,000. `bfgs` keeps an `n × n` matrix. The reduction test of the least-squares fits says that the cost cannot be lowered by more than `rtol` of itself, not that the gradient is small: with the default `rtol = 4√ε` it accepts a point whose cosines are up to `2.4·10⁻⁴`, twice what MINPACK's default `ftol` allows. On the decay fit with an offset that left the parameters about `10⁻⁷` from the minimiser, as MINPACK's defaults do; on the audit's sampling of the twenty NIST problems, 40 starts each within ±50% and ±90% of the certified values, `levenberg_marquardt` stopped so at a cost more than 0.1% above the certified one with a cosine above `10⁻⁴` in 20 and 10 of 800 runs (MINPACK 10 and 15), at ENSO and Gauss1 mostly, none of them with a cosine above `10⁻³`. Smaller `rtol` and `gtol` go further. L-BFGS-B's reduction test, the reference code's, likewise says that `f` has stopped improving, which on an ill-conditioned problem can be far from the minimiser: on the first review's 260 random quadratics with the default `ftol` the median error of a `Reduction` stop was `10⁻⁶` of the largest coordinate below condition 30 and `3·10⁻⁴` above `3·10³`, `0.5` at worst, where a `ProjectedGradient` stop was never off by more than `2·10⁻⁶`; in `f32`, with `ftol = 10ε`, `2·10⁻⁴` (at worst `7·10⁻⁴`) below condition 30, `2·10⁻³` up to `3·10³`, and again `0.5` at worst beyond. On functions that are not quadratics an `f32` `Reduction` stop can be further off: on the second review's 800 random problems a log-sum-exp with a ridge in 40 variables with a memory of 1 stopped so with `f` 0.6% above its minimum and a projected gradient of 0.44, and coupled double wells of 40 variables 1.3% above; with `ftol = 0` the first of them ends instead with `LineSearch` and `converged` false, `3·10⁻⁵` above. `LbfgsbResult::projected_gradient` tells how far the first-order conditions are from holding, and `ftol = 0` leaves only the gradient test. `pgtol` is absolute, as in the reference code, so that a function scaled down far enough passes it at its start; and a function so small that `x − ∇f(x)` is `x` in floating point cannot be started within bounds at all: the first step, down the gradient with the model `B = I`, moves nothing, and the method ends at once with `LbfgsbStop::LineSearch` (SciPy reports convergence from the same point). L-BFGS-B, with any `ftol`, stops with `LbfgsbStop::LineSearch` where the rounding of `f` hides the decrease of every step before the projected gradient is down to `pgtol`; SciPy takes a further step there and reports convergence. With `ftol = 0` that is 11 of the reference problems below; with `ftol > 0` it was 2 of the second review's 800 random problems, quadratics of 2 and 3 variables of condition `1.2·10⁵` and `8·10⁷` with `pgtol = 10⁻⁹`, left at projected gradients of `8·10⁻⁴` and `2·10⁻⁵` where SciPy's further step reaches `9·10⁻⁸` and `4·10⁻⁹`, `f` equal to an ulp. In `f32` a gradient tolerance of `√ε` cannot always be met: where `f` is computed with cancellation its values stop telling points apart first, and the gradient methods then say `converged` false, two searches after their last step. On a quadratic of condition `10⁵` or more the conjugate gradient may still be lowering `f` a little at every step when `max_iter` runs out: 4 of the second review's 60 random `f32` quadratics with a budget of 5,000 steps.

## Linear programming

`LinearProgram` finds the point that makes a linear objective as small, or as large, as it can be under linear inequalities, linear equations and bounds on each variable:

```text
minimise (or maximise)   cᵀx
subject to               A_le·x ≤ b_le,   A_eq·x = b_eq,   l ≤ x ≤ u
```

| Item | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| `LinearProgram::minimize(&c)`, `LinearProgram::maximize(&c)` | the programme of the costs `c`, every variable `x ≥ 0` until told otherwise |
| `.subject_to_le(&a, &b)`, `.subject_to_eq(&a, &b)` | adds the rows `a·x ≤ b` or `a·x = b` (a dense `Matrix`, a slice); each may be called more than once, and a matrix of no rows adds nothing |
| `.bounds(&lower, &upper)` | the bounds of every variable; either may be infinite (a free variable, a one-sided bound), and equal bounds fix a variable |
| `.solve()`, `.solve_with(&LpOptions)` | the simplex method; `None` for data that are not finite, bounds that mean nothing, a tolerance that is not positive, or an answer that could not be verified |
| `LpOptions` | `tol` (`ε^(4/7)`, about `10⁻⁹` for `f64` and `10⁻⁴` for `f32`), `max_iter` (10,000 pivots), `stall_limit` (100), `scaling` (on) |
| `LpResult` | `status`, `x`, `objective`, `slack` (`b_le − A_le·x`), `dual_le`, `dual_eq`, `reduced_costs`, `pivots` |
| `LpStatus` | `Optimal`, `Infeasible`, `Unbounded`, `IterationLimit` |

An inequality `a·x ≥ b` is given as `−a·x ≤ −b`. The default bounds are those of `scipy.optimize.linprog`, and so is the sign convention of the dual values: each is the rate at which the optimum changes with the right-hand side of its row, or with the bound its variable sits at. For a minimisation the multipliers of the inequalities are `≤ 0`, a reduced cost `r = c − A_leᵀy_le − A_eqᵀy_eq` is `≥ 0` at a lower bound and `≤ 0` at an upper one; for a maximisation every sign is reversed. A multiplier is zero where its row is slack and a reduced cost, to its tolerance, where its variable is strictly inside its bounds, and the objective equals `b_leᵀy_le + b_eqᵀy_eq + rᵀx`.

The method is the primal simplex method in two phases, in the revised form, on bounded variables (Dantzig's upper-bounding technique; Chvátal, *Linear Programming*, 1983; Maros, *Computational Techniques of the Simplex Method*, 2003). The revised form was chosen over a tableau because the issue builds on `lu` and because it recomputes the basic solution from the data at every factorisation, which a tableau cannot.

- Every row has a logical variable, a slack `z ≥ 0` for an inequality and `z = 0` for an equation, and the logical variables are the first basis; an equation that repeats others leaves its logical variable in the basis at zero, so a rank-deficient `A_eq` does not stop phase one. The rows and the columns are scaled by powers of two until the largest element of each is near one, and the right-hand sides and the bounds by the power of two nearest the reciprocal of the median right-hand side; a scale factor may be any power of two whose reciprocal is a normal number too, so rows in units of `10⁻³⁰⁰` are scaled as well as rows in units of `10⁻³`, and no digit of the data changes. Every other variable starts as near zero as its bounds allow, so a bound of `10³⁰` written for an infinite one never enters the arithmetic.
- A variable out of the basis sits at a bound (or at zero inside them), and the entering variable may run to its own bound without a change of basis: no bound becomes a row.
- Phase one is the composite method: the basic variables that break a bound get the cost `±1`, the sum of the amounts they break their bounds by is minimised, and a variable that comes back to its bound stops there. Phase two minimises the objective from the feasible basis.
- The basis is factorised by `Matrix::lu`, each change of basis is one factor of the product form of the inverse (Dantzig and Orchard-Hays, 1954), and the basis is factorised again, its basic solution recomputed from the data with a step of iterative refinement, every twenty changes and before any answer is given.
- The multipliers `y`, the solution of `Bᵀy = c_B`, get a step of iterative refinement too, and the residual left bounds their error where it matters: the reduced cost the true multipliers of the basis give differs from the one `y` gives by `(B⁻¹a_j)ᵀ(c_B − Bᵀy)`, so the column of a variable as the basis sees it bounds the error of its reduced cost. Multipliers that are large, or wrong, in rows a variable does not meet do not enter its bound.
- Pricing is Dantzig's rule. Once `stall_limit` pivots in a row have left the objective where it was, Bland's rule (Bland, 1977) takes over until it moves again, so the method cannot cycle. Pricing proposes on an estimate of each reduced cost's error, from what the refinement changed it by; the variable it proposes enters only if its reduced cost is beyond the bound its column gives, and computed again from that column, `c_q − c_Bᵀ·B⁻¹a_q`, has the same sign. A reduced cost within its error is rounding, not a direction of descent.
- The ratio test is Harris's two-pass test (Harris, 1973): bounds relaxed by a tenth of their tolerance, then the largest pivot among the rows that block within that step; an element of the entering column below the smaller of the tolerance and `ε^(4/7)`, times the larger of one and the column's largest element, is never pivoted on.

Every status but `IterationLimit` is verified against the data before it is returned, on a basis factorised afresh. `Optimal`: the point, as it is reported, with every variable inside its bounds, satisfies the rows; no reduced cost has the sign that would improve the objective by more than its tolerance and its error bound — a variable that pricing passed over and that this check finds enters, and the method goes on. That makes the multipliers a feasible point of the dual programme, to the tolerance, and by duality no feasible point has an objective below the dual objective `bᵀy + Σ_j d_j·β_j`, with `β_j` the bound the sign of `d_j` prices. The objective is also checked to equal that dual objective within what the rows' residuals, held to the tolerance, are worth at `y`, a check that the two sides were computed consistently: the first two parts imply it up to rounding. `Infeasible`: the multipliers of phase one are a Farkas certificate, a combination of the rows that the box of the bounds cannot satisfy, with the same check of the reduced costs and a margin larger than half the tolerance and than what the multipliers' own error could make of it. `Unbounded`: a feasible point and a ray from it that keeps every row and bound and along which the objective falls. An answer that fails its certificate, or a basis with a column that is to rounding a combination of the others, gives `None`. The tolerance is relative throughout: a bound to `max(1, |bound|)`, the slack of a row and the row itself to the size of the row's terms `|b_i| + Σ|a_ij·x_j|`, a reduced cost to `|c_j| + |a_jᵀy|`, the two numbers it is the difference of. A basic variable is held to its bounds no more tightly than the error its own value carries: at each factorisation the residual `r` of the rows at the point, and the rounding `γ·t` it is computed with, are taken through the magnitudes of the inverse of the basis, `|B⁻¹|·(|r| + γ·t)`, twice over. That follows the coupling of the basis, so one large right-hand side, slack or value loosens only the variables the basis computes from it, and not the rows and bounds of the others; the ratio test keeps to the tolerance alone, and the rows are checked at the point as it is reported.

```rust
use a::math::matrix::Matrix;
use a::math::numerics::{LinearProgram, LpOptions, LpStatus};

// Maximise 3x + 2y + 4z subject to x + y + 2z ≤ 7, 2x + z ≤ 5 and x + y + z = 5, with
// 0 ≤ x ≤ 2, y free and z ≥ 0.
let inf = f64::INFINITY;
let lp = LinearProgram::maximize(&[3.0, 2.0, 4.0])
    .subject_to_le(&Matrix::from_rows(&[[1.0, 1.0, 2.0], [2.0, 0.0, 1.0]]), &[7.0, 5.0])
    .subject_to_eq(&Matrix::from_rows(&[[1.0, 1.0, 1.0]]), &[5.0])
    .bounds(&[0.0, -inf, 0.0], &[2.0, inf, inf]);
let r = lp.solve().unwrap();
assert_eq!(r.status, LpStatus::Optimal);
assert!((r.objective - 15.5).abs() < 1e-12);
for (x, expected) in r.x.iter().zip([1.5, 1.5, 2.0]) {
    assert!((x - expected).abs() < 1e-12);
}
// Each dual value is the rate at which the maximum grows with its right-hand side, and
// together they price the objective exactly: strong duality.
assert!((r.dual_le[0] - 1.5).abs() < 1e-12 && (r.dual_le[1] - 0.5).abs() < 1e-12);
assert!((r.dual_eq[0] - 0.5).abs() < 1e-12);
let dual = 7.0 * r.dual_le[0] + 5.0 * r.dual_le[1] + 5.0 * r.dual_eq[0];
assert!((r.objective - dual).abs() < 1e-12);

// Bland's rule throughout reaches the same optimum; x ≥ 3 contradicts the bound x ≤ 2.
let bland = lp.solve_with(&LpOptions::new().with_stall_limit(0)).unwrap();
assert!((bland.objective - 15.5).abs() < 1e-12);
let r = lp.subject_to_le(&Matrix::from_rows(&[[-1.0, 0.0, 0.0]]), &[-3.0]).solve().unwrap();
assert_eq!(r.status, LpStatus::Infeasible);
```

### Limits

The matrices and the factorisation are dense: a pivot costs `O(m² + m·n)`, and the basis is factorised afresh, at `O(m³)` (and as much again for the magnitudes of its inverse), every twenty pivots and before every answer, which suits up to a few hundred rows and columns; there is no presolve, no dual simplex and no warm start. Dantzig's rule is exponential in the worst case and Bland's slower still in practice; a cycle can run for `stall_limit` pivots before Bland's rule takes over, unless a refactorisation's rounding breaks it first, as it does on Chvátal's example at the twentieth. The tolerances of the bounds and the rows have a floor of one after the scaling, so where the right-hand sides span many orders of magnitude the smallest are held only to `tol` times the median, and a programme infeasible or unbounded by less than the tolerance may be reported feasible or bounded. In particular a reduced cost within its tolerance is taken for zero even when the bound it points to is infinite: a programme unbounded only along rays whose slope is within the tolerance of the costs' terms is reported optimal (seen for rays of a relative slope of 2.8·10⁻¹⁰ at the default tolerance), and beside multipliers of `10⁴` an optimum was 2.3·10⁻⁸ above the exact one, about 2·10⁻¹¹ of the objective's terms. Without the scaling the tolerances are in the units of the data, which for rows in very small units are no longer small, and the status or the optimum can then be right only for them. Where the rounding of a step is larger than the tolerance of the bounds it moves variables towards, as on the cubes of Klee and Minty above, the point computed afresh at a factorisation can break a bound that phase two was keeping; phase one resumes, and after the tenth time the answer is `None`. On an ill-conditioned programme the point can move much further than the tolerance: two equations that differ by `δ` fix a variable only to the rows' tolerance over `δ`, and reduced costs made of terms of `1/δ` are known only to their rounding; the dual values still bound every feasible objective, so an optimum is never above the exact one by more than that rounding. In `f32` the tolerance is `10⁻⁴`. The dual values of a degenerate optimum are those of the final basis, one of many.

## Interpolation and differentiation

Piecewise cubics through data, one polynomial or one rational function over a whole interval, and derivatives from values. Everything is generic over `f32` and `f64`; AAA takes complex samples too.

| Function | What it gives |
| --- | --- |
| `Spline::cubic`, `cubic_clamped`, `cubic_not_a_knot` | the `C²` cubic splines: natural, clamped to given end slopes, not-a-knot |
| `Spline::akima` | Akima's `C¹` spline, which follows straight runs and does not ring at steps |
| `Spline::pchip` | the shape-preserving `C¹` spline: monotone between monotone knots |
| `chebyshev` | the Chebyshev interpolant of a given degree of a function on `[a, b]`, as a series |
| `chebyshev_nodes(n, a, b)` | the `n` zeros of the Chebyshev polynomial `T[n]` on `[a, b]`, in increasing order: nodes for barycentric interpolation |
| `barycentric_weights`, `barycentric_interpolate` | the polynomial through values at any distinct nodes, in the second barycentric form |
| `aaa`, `aaa_with`, `aaa_complex`, `aaa_complex_with` | a rational approximation of samples on the line or in the complex plane, with its poles, residues and zeros |
| `derivative`, `derivative_with`, `second_derivative`, `second_derivative_with` | the first and second derivative of a function at a point, by Richardson extrapolation |

**Splines.** The five share the Hermite form — on every interval the cubic that takes `y[i]` with slope `m[i]` at one knot and `y[i+1]` with slope `m[i+1]` at the next — and differ in how the slopes are chosen. The natural and clamped splines solve the tridiagonal system that makes the second derivative continuous, the end conditions eliminated into its first and last rows, which leaves it diagonally dominant. The not-a-knot conditions make one cubic of the first two intervals and one of the last two, so the not-a-knot spline is solved as the cubic spline of the knots without the second and the penultimate one that passes through those two points too: the slopes at its knots solve a tridiagonal system whose end rows say that the end cubics pass through the two points left out, by Gaussian elimination with scaled partial pivoting, and the slopes at the two points are those of the end cubics. Through four knots it is the cubic through them, its slopes from divided differences; through three the parabola; through two (like the natural spline) the line, while the clamped spline through two knots is the cubic with the two given slopes. The spline is that of C. de Boor's *A Practical Guide to Splines* (1978) and SciPy's `CubicSpline`, which write the system in all `n` slopes with the conditions combined into its first and last rows; those rows lose as many digits as a short second or penultimate interval is shorter than its neighbours (the square of that through four knots), where the data themselves lose far fewer. Akima's slopes (H. Akima, *J. ACM* 17, 1970) are weighted means of the two secants at a knot, each weighted by how far apart the two secants beyond the other are, with Akima's own continuation of the secants past both ends — the rule of SciPy's `Akima1DInterpolator`; a weight is zero here only when two secants are equal, where SciPy takes a weight below `10⁻⁹` of the largest as zero. PCHIP (F. N. Fritsch and R. E. Carlson, *SIAM J. Numer. Anal.* 17, 1980, with the slopes of Fritsch and Butland, *SIAM J. Sci. Stat. Comput.* 5, 1984, as SLATEC's `PCHIM` and SciPy's `PchipInterpolator`) puts zero at an interior knot where the data turn and the weighted harmonic mean of the secants elsewhere, and at an end the one-sided slope of the parabola through three knots, zero if it points against the end secant and at most three times it if the data turn next to the end. Neither forms a product of two secants, so `f32` data of size `1e20` or `1e-23` are handled like any other. A `Spline` answers `evaluate`, `derivative`, `second_derivative` and `integral(a, b)` between its first and last knot, finding the interval by bisection, and `None` outside them: it does not extrapolate. Knots or values that are not finite give `None`; knots of the wrong shape (lengths that differ, fewer than two, not increasing) panic.

**Chebyshev and barycentric interpolation.** `chebyshev(f, a, b, n)` interpolates `f` at the `n + 1` zeros of the Chebyshev polynomial `T[n+1]` on `[a, b]` — the construction of `numpy.polynomial.chebyshev.Chebyshev.interpolate` — takes the coefficients by the discrete cosine transform of the values (summed directly: `(n + 1)²` cosines), and evaluates the series, its derivative and its integral by Clenshaw's recurrence. The degree is the caller's: nothing chooses it or estimates the error. Outside `[a, b]` the series is evaluated as the polynomial it is, which is an extrapolation. `barycentric_weights` computes `1/Π(x[k] − x[j])` for any distinct nodes with every difference split exactly into a significand and a power of two, so that the weights stay in range for any number of nodes on any interval (J.-P. Berrut and L. N. Trefethen, *SIAM Review* 46, 2004, scale the differences for the same reason); `barycentric_interpolate` evaluates the second barycentric formula, with the differences in units of the span of the nodes, and gives the datum itself at a node and next to one. The weights cost a pass over the pairs of nodes; whether the polynomial is good depends on the nodes — at Chebyshev nodes it converges, at equally spaced ones it does not.

**Rational approximation (AAA).** The algorithm of Y. Nakatsukasa, O. Sete and L. N. Trefethen (*SIAM J. Sci. Comput.* 40, 2018) builds `r(x) = Σ w_j·f_j/(x − z_j) ÷ Σ w_j/(x − z_j)` greedily: each step adds as a support point the sample where the approximation is worst, and takes the weights from the right singular vector of the smallest singular value of the Loewner matrix `(f_i − f_j)/(x_i − z_j)` on the other samples (`Matrix::svd`). It stops when the error on the samples is at most `rtol·max|f|` or at the term limit; `converged()` says which, `error()` is the error reached and `errors()` its history. The samples are prepared and the steps taken as SciPy's `scipy.interpolate.AAA` takes them — non-finite samples dropped, points sorted and repeated ones dropped (the first value kept), the start from the mean, the first of equally bad samples, singular vectors summed when the smallest singular value is repeated, the columns of the Loewner matrix scaled to unit norm once its condition number passes `1/(3ε)` — so that the two choose the same support points. Before anything is computed the values are divided by the power of two that leaves the largest of them between 1 and 2, and what is reported in their units (values, residues, errors) is multiplied back, so that the approximation of `2^k·f` is that of `f`, the same support points, weights, poles and zeros bit for bit, for every `k` that keeps the values normal numbers; the 2-norms are summed after the same kind of scaling. Two things differ from SciPy: at most `⌈M/2⌉` support points are used for `M` samples, since beyond that the weights are no longer determined by the data (so with few samples it can stop short of the tolerance where SciPy interpolates them all), and when the term limit is reached the step of smallest error is returned, not the last. `evaluate` gives the datum at a support point exactly, and `evaluate_complex` evaluates anywhere in the complex plane. The poles are the finite eigenvalues of the paper's arrowhead pencil: with a shift `s` away from the data they are `s + 1/μ` for the eigenvalues `μ` of `diag(t) − t·(w∘t)ᵀ/Σw_j t_j`, `t_j = 1/(z_j − s)`, other than its structural zero, which is removed exactly by restricting the matrix to `{v : wᵀv = 0}` through a Householder basis; the standard eigenproblem left is solved by `Matrix::eigenvalues`. Its eigenvalues are only estimates: the reduction divides by `Σw_j t_j = −d(s)`, which is small at every shift when the denominator is small away from the samples, as it is for an approximation of an entire function, whose poles are spurious — there they can be off by more than the distance between neighbouring poles. They are refined together by the Aberth–Ehrlich iteration (O. Aberth, *Math. Comp.* 27, 1973) on the polynomial `d(x)·Π(x − z_j)`, which has the same zeros and no pole at a support point, written about the support point of the largest term so that it stays finite next to one, and each is polished by Newton's method on it, a step kept only while it lowers the backward error — the size of the relative changes of the weights and of the point itself that make it an exact zero. For real samples real poles stay real and pairs stay conjugate in that iteration, and two real estimates that do not settle are tried as the conjugate pair the eigenvalue routine may have split. Then every pole is checked, and what is not a zero to rounding, or is one zero found twice, is started again, up to six times: the poles found are held fixed, so that the iteration keeps the others away from them, and the others are iterated freely in the complex plane — so that a conjugate pair the estimates gave where the denominator has two real zeros can part, and two real estimates can meet as a pair — first from where they were, then from points on circles round the data. What is returned is what passed the check: every pole has a backward error of at most `8ε` (`ε` of `f64`), is given once — two within twice the distance that bound allows from a zero of each other are one found twice when the slope of the polynomial points the same way at both and no other pole is near enough to turn it; at a pole numerically double it points opposite ways, and both are given — and for real samples is real or one of an exact conjugate pair; a pole no restart brings within the bound is left out, so that fewer than `m − 1` are returned. All of this is done in `f64` for `f32` samples too, which are `f64` numbers exactly, the poles rounded to `f32` at the end, since the reduction can lose more digits than `f32` has. The zeros are found the same way from `w∘f`, together with the support points of value zero, and the residues are `n(p)/d′(p)`. The clean-up, on by default, is the paper's: poles whose residue, divided by their distance from the samples, is below `clean_up_tol` (`1e−13`) times the geometric mean of the nonzero `|f|` are Froissart doublets; the support point nearest each is removed and the weights solved for again, once.

**Differentiation.** Central difference quotients of the steps `h, h/2, h/4, …` are combined by Neville's scheme, each column cancelling the next even power of `h`, and the table's own error estimate (C. J. F. Ridders, *Adv. Eng. Software* 4, 1982) decides where round-off takes over. The steps descend from `0.37·max(|x|, 1)`, and for `0 < |x| < 1` again from `0.37·|x|`, so that a function singular at 0 is differentiated at `1e−100`; they stop at round-off, at 16 ulps of `x`, or after 64 steps. An answer must pass three checks: the estimated error plus the round-off of the values is at most `tol` times the size of the derivative or of the one-sided slopes around it, whichever is larger (at or near a stationary point, the slopes); the one-sided quotients agree in the limit (a kink, a jump or a pole gives `None`, not the symmetric quotient's mean of two slopes); and three more quotients, at steps `√2`, `2^(1/3)` and `2^(3/4)` times the finest used — off the ladder of halvings — agree with the table's prediction to the tolerance. A periodic function sampled at steps of many periods can still pass by chance — on the halvings of a step of `2^j` periods it takes the values of a slower sinusoid, and at such steps its quotients are small numbers of the size of the reciprocal step, among which a loose tolerance is met — so the ladder does not stop at the first answer that passes: it keeps the one of smallest estimated error relative to its size and goes on while a finer step could do better, and when a finer step shows quotients far larger than that answer's, the function varying on a scale its steps did not resolve, and no better answer follows, there is none. Last, the values at the floats next to `x` may move from `f(x)` by no more than a quarter of what they moved at the answer's finest step, which is at least 16 floats wide; a function oscillating on a scale between the two does not pass. A function whose values do not change from steps where their round-off is below the tolerance down to steps where it is above has the derivative zero. A rung whose round-off estimate has underflowed — the steps so large beside the values that it falls below the smallest subnormal number, every quotient then below the smallest normal one — gives no answer, since its relative tests would read `0 ≤ 0`; the round-off of a second difference, `a/h²`, is formed as `a/h/h` when `h²` overflows, so that it underflows only where it truly is that small. `DiffOptions` holds the first step (`0`: chosen as above), `tol` (`√ε` for `derivative`; `second_derivative` uses `ε^(1/3)`, as the round-off of a second difference grows like `1/h²`) and the number of columns (8).

```rust
use a::math::numerics::{Spline, aaa, chebyshev, derivative, second_derivative};

// A not-a-knot spline through eight irregular samples of the sine, and its integral.
let x = [0.0, 0.3, 0.7, 1.1, 1.6, 2.0, 2.6, 3.0];
let y: Vec<f64> = x.iter().map(|t: &f64| t.sin()).collect();
let s = Spline::cubic_not_a_knot(&x, &y).unwrap();
assert!((s.evaluate(1.3).unwrap() - 1.3f64.sin()).abs() < 2e-3);
assert!((s.integral(0.0, 3.0).unwrap() - (1.0 - 3.0f64.cos())).abs() < 2e-3);
assert_eq!(s.evaluate(3.5), None);
// PCHIP keeps a flat run flat.
let monotone = Spline::pchip(&[0.0, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0], &[0.0, 0.1, 0.1, 1.0]).unwrap();
assert_eq!(monotone.evaluate(1.5), Some(0.1));

// The Chebyshev interpolant of degree 12 of eˣ on [0, 1], and its integral.
let c = chebyshev(|t: f64| t.exp(), 0.0, 1.0, 12).unwrap();
assert!((c.evaluate(0.3) - 0.3f64.exp()).abs() < 1e-15);
assert!((c.integral(1.0) - (core::f64::consts::E - 1.0)).abs() < 1e-15);

// AAA: tan(πx/2) from 1000 samples inside (-1, 1), with its poles at ±1 to rounding.
let xs: Vec<f64> = (0..1000).map(|k| -1.0 + (2.0 * f64::from(k) + 1.0) / 1000.0).collect();
let fs: Vec<f64> = xs.iter().map(|t| (core::f64::consts::FRAC_PI_2 * t).tan()).collect();
let r = aaa(&xs, &fs).unwrap();
assert!(r.converged() && r.degree() == 7);
assert!((r.evaluate(0.9991) - (core::f64::consts::FRAC_PI_2 * 0.9991).tan()).abs() < 1e-8);
assert!(r.poles().iter().any(|p| (p.re - 1.0).abs() < 1e-14 && p.im == 0.0));

// Derivatives: to the tolerance, or nothing.
let d = derivative(|t: f64| t.sin(), 1.0).unwrap();
assert!((d - 1.0f64.cos()).abs() < 1e-14);
assert!((second_derivative(|t: f64| t.ln(), 1e-100).unwrap() + 1e200).abs() < 1e192);
assert_eq!(derivative(|t: f64| (t - 0.3).abs(), 0.3), None);
```

### Limits

A spline does not extrapolate, and Chebyshev and barycentric polynomials extrapolate as polynomials. A Chebyshev fit's degree is the caller's. Between its samples an AAA approximation can be much worse than on them near a singularity of the data — `|x|` from 1000 samples is 6.7·10⁻¹³ off on the samples and 4.2·10⁻⁴ between them next to 0. Past the accuracy the arithmetic allows (a tolerance of 0, or below `ε^(3/4)`), the steps of AAA depend on the last bits of the decompositions, which differ between debug and optimised builds and between platforms, and so do the support points and doublets found there. On any data the choice of a support point among samples whose errors differ in the last bit only (symmetric data) is a tie that the platform's rounding breaks, and the paths part there; and the spurious poles of an approximation of an entire function rest on the last bits of its weights, and lie elsewhere on each platform (those beyond the samples of `cos(5.18πx)` at 267 Chebyshev points at −3.893 and 3.479 on aarch64, at −5.569 and 4.278 on x86_64). The clean-up solves for the weights once more and can raise the error past the attainable accuracy by two orders of magnitude (`|x|` above); the clean-up is one pass and can leave a doublet. `error()` and `converged()` rest on the error as computed in the type's arithmetic, as SciPy's do: of 300 cases a review recomputed exactly, one called converged had an error of 1.004 times the tolerance. `poles()` and `zeros()` give only zeros they have checked to be zeros to rounding: one that no restart brings within the bound is left out, and where zeros lie closer together than rounding can resolve (a cluster, a zero of high multiplicity) the rule that takes two for one zero found twice rests on the slope of the polynomial and can be wrong; neither happened in the cases measured. On an interval only a few floats wide, Chebyshev nodes can coincide (50 on `[10⁶, 10⁶ + 1]` in `f32`), and the barycentric weights of coinciding nodes panic. Akima's slope at a knot between two runs that are straight to within rounding follows the rounding, anywhere between the two secants (0.667 for secants 0 and 1 carrying noise of `10⁻¹³`), where SciPy, which calls weights below `10⁻⁹` of the largest zero, takes their mean. A derivative is that of the function as it is computed: features narrower than the steps tried are invisible (of 4000 staircases `floor(N·x)/N`, one gave the slope 1 of their envelope), where the spacing `u` of the floats near `x` (of which every step is a multiple) is close to a multiple of the function's period (the sine at `10¹⁰` in `f32`, where the floats are 1024 apart, 163 periods less 0.03 of one) or is many periods long (the sine beyond about `10¹⁷` in `f64`), the function takes at every float the values of a slower one, of slope about `(u mod period)/u`, and is indistinguishable from it — of the sine's slope at 100 points of `[10²³⁴, 10²³⁵]`, 11 are given, of size `10⁻²²³`, and of its curvature at 100 points of `[10¹¹¹, 10¹¹²]`, 7, of size `10⁻¹⁹⁶` — rounding of the function's own argument that is smooth on the scale of the steps passes the checks as part of the function (`sin(ωx + φ)` computed as written, `ω·x` from `10⁶` to `10¹³` in `f64`: 10 of 562 answers off by more than ten tolerances of `ω`), a curvature below the resolution of the values at the largest step is given as zero, and at or near a stationary point the accuracy is relative to the slopes around it, which can be many times the derivative (`cosh(0.0103·x)′` at `−1.03·10⁻⁶` is 5.5·10⁻⁶ off relatively at the tolerance 1.5·10⁻⁸, the one-sided slopes at the answer's steps, 0.18 and 0.092, being 4.5·10⁴ times the derivative; in `f32` the slope 2.3·10⁻⁷ of `1/(1 − 0.076·x²)` at 1.5·10⁻⁶ comes out as 0, the values at `x ± h` being equal as computed at the answer's steps, 0.18 and 0.092, where the one-sided slopes are 7.0·10⁻³). Where the steps are so large beside the values that the round-off estimate of the quotients underflows, a derivative smaller than the smallest normal number gives `None`: the curvature of `ln` at `10¹⁷⁰`, `−10⁻³⁴⁰`, and of `sin(x/10¹⁶⁰)` at `10¹⁶⁰`, the subnormal `−8.4·10⁻³²¹`, which was given to its few bits — and so do all second derivatives of bounded functions at `|x|` from about `10¹⁷⁶`, where the squares of the steps leave the range of `f64`.
